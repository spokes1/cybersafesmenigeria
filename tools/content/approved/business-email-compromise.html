Business Email Compromise

Understand how criminals impersonate suppliers and business contacts, change payment instructions and use convincing email conversations to steal money.

The email looks familiar. But are the payment instructions genuine?

Your business has been communicating with a supplier for several days.

You have discussed prices, agreed on delivery and received an invoice.

Just before you make the transfer, another email arrives.

"Please use our new account details. Our previous account is temporarily unavailable."

The message includes the correct invoice reference. It appears to come from the supplier, and it may even be part of the original email conversation.

Would your staff make the payment?

This is the kind of situation criminals exploit through Business Email Compromise (BEC).

The danger is not always an obviously suspicious link or attachment. Sometimes it is a believable instruction inside a conversation your business already trusts.

The most useful protection is a clear rule for checking payment changes before money leaves your account.

Rule to remember

AN EMAIL CONVERSATION IS NOT ENOUGH TO CONFIRM A CHANGE IN BANK DETAILS. VERIFY THROUGH A CONTACT METHOD YOU ALREADY TRUST.

WHAT IS BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE?

Business Email Compromise, commonly called BEC, is a type of fraud in which criminals misuse business email identities or conversations to trick people into sending money, disclosing information or carrying out unauthorised actions.

A criminal may impersonate:

  • A supplier sending an invoice.
  • A business owner asking an employee to make a payment.
  • An accountant sharing updated bank details.
  • A customer requesting a change to an order or delivery arrangement.
  • A business partner requesting confidential documents.

Sometimes the criminal has gained access to a genuine email account.

In other cases, the account has not been hacked. The criminal may create a similar-looking email address, falsify the displayed sender identity or pose as someone familiar.

This distinction matters.

A message can be fraudulent even when the email address looks correct, and a suspicious message does not automatically prove an account was compromised.

How is BEC different from ordinary phishing?

Phishing often tries to make someone click a harmful link, open a dangerous attachment or reveal login details.

BEC frequently uses a more specific approach.

The criminal may refer to a genuine transaction, a known business relationship or a real employee's responsibilities.

The message may contain no links or attachments at all.

For example, a phishing message might claim that your email account will be disabled unless you sign in immediately.

A BEC message might claim that your regular supplier has changed the bank account for an invoice you were already expecting.

The two threats can also be connected. A criminal may first steal email login details through phishing and later use that access to commit BEC.

WHY SHOULD A NIGERIAN SMALL BUSINESS CARE?

Your business already relies on trusted conversations

Many small businesses use email to exchange quotations, invoices, purchase orders and payment confirmations.

Regular communication helps businesses operate efficiently, but it can also create opportunities for impersonation.

A fraudulent instruction may concern a real transaction

A criminal does not always need to invent a supplier or customer.

If they gain access to genuine correspondence, they may learn about a payment that is already being prepared.

A convincing email can put employees under pressure

A message may claim that payment must be completed before a delivery deadline or that a senior manager has authorised an exception.

Staff may feel pressured to act quickly.

Your business can be impersonated too

BEC is not only about money leaving your account.

A criminal may impersonate your business and send fraudulent payment instructions to customers.

This can damage customer trust and create disputes over unpaid invoices.

Technical protection alone is not enough

Strong email security reduces risk, but a convincing fraudulent payment request may still arrive.

That is why payment verification procedures matter alongside account protection.

HOW BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE HAPPENS

Stage 1: The criminal gains credibility

The criminal may obtain access to a genuine mailbox through stolen credentials or another account compromise.

Alternatively, they may create a lookalike address or impersonate a known employee or supplier.

They might know the names of people involved in a transaction, the type of goods being purchased or an expected payment date.

Stage 2: A business conversation becomes the opportunity

The criminal sends or inserts a message at a point where someone is about to act.

The message may refer to:

  • An outstanding supplier invoice.
  • A scheduled payment.
  • A purchase order awaiting approval.
  • An employee salary-account change.
  • An urgent request supposedly from management.

If a mailbox is compromised, the criminal may also read messages or create forwarding and filtering rules to monitor communications or conceal activity.

However, not every BEC attempt involves mailbox access or hidden forwarding rules.

Stage 3: The instruction changes

The message introduces a new request.

For example:

"Our accounts department has moved to another bank. Kindly update the beneficiary details before payment."

The account details may direct the transfer to the criminal or another account used in the fraud.

A different variation may request confidential documents or an unauthorised payment on behalf of a manager.

Stage 4: The employee is encouraged to act without checking

The criminal may ask for urgency, secrecy or an exception to normal procedures.

Sometimes the message discourages phone calls or claims the person who usually approves payments is unavailable.

If the employee relies on the email alone, the business may send money or information to the wrong recipient.

Stage 5: The problem becomes apparent

A business might discover the fraud when the legitimate supplier follows up about an unpaid invoice.

Alternatively, staff may notice an unusual mailbox rule, unfamiliar sign-in or suspicious correspondence.

By that point, a fraudulent transfer may already have taken place.

A prompt response matters because financial institutions may have limited opportunities to assist after funds have moved.

THREE WAYS A FRAUDULENT EMAIL CAN LOOK GENUINE

1. A lookalike email address

The message appears to come from a familiar supplier, but the actual sender address contains a subtle difference.

This could involve a changed letter, an extra character or an unfamiliar domain.

Checking the complete sender address may help identify the problem.

However, a familiar-looking address is not proof that a message is genuine.

2. A compromised real email account

A criminal uses an account belonging to a genuine employee or business contact.

The message may come from the correct address and appear inside an existing conversation.

In this situation, checking the sender address alone may not reveal the fraud.

Independent verification of important instructions is still necessary.

3. A familiar displayed sender name

The inbox shows a name such as "Accounts Department" or the name of a manager, but the underlying address belongs to someone else.

This is particularly easy to overlook on a phone, where the email application may initially display only the sender's name.

Inspecting the full address can help, but payment verification remains essential.

WARNING SIGNS WORTH INVESTIGATING

Unexpected changes to bank details

A supplier asks you to use a different account just before payment.

This is a strong reason to pause and verify, even if the request turns out to be legitimate.

Sudden urgency

A message demands immediate payment and claims there will be serious consequences if you delay.

Unusual secrecy

Someone asks an employee not to discuss a transfer with colleagues or not to follow the usual approval procedure.

Requests outside normal responsibilities

A staff member receives an unexpected instruction to transfer money, send payroll information or purchase items on behalf of management.

Slightly different sender details

The sender's full email address, reply-to address or domain does not match the trusted contact record.

Unexplained email-account changes

You notice unfamiliar forwarding rules, recovery details, connected applications, sign-in alerts or messages in the sent and deleted folders.

These signs may indicate account compromise and should be investigated.

Remember: A BEC message may contain none of these obvious warning signs. A genuine-looking conversation still requires independent confirmation when payment instructions change.

HOW TO VERIFY AN UNUSUAL PAYMENT REQUEST

This is the most important procedure in the article.

Step 1: Pause the transaction

Do not rush to pay simply because the message appears to come from a familiar person.

Keep the payment pending while the information is checked.

Step 2: Use an existing contact record

Find the supplier's telephone number or another authorised contact method from a record you already trust.

This might be the number recorded when the supplier relationship was established, an existing supplier register or another independently verified source.

Do not rely only on the telephone number or contact details in the email requesting the change.

Step 3: Confirm the specific instruction

Ask the supplier's authorised representative whether the payment details have changed.

Confirm the relevant invoice and beneficiary information using the business's established verification procedure.

For unusually sensitive or high-value changes, require additional approval according to your business's rules.

Step 4: Make a decision

If the change is independently confirmed and the normal payment approvals are satisfied, the business can proceed.

If the information cannot be verified, keep the payment on hold and escalate it to the business owner or authorised manager.

There is no need to accuse the supplier of fraud. The immediate objective is to avoid sending money to an unverified account.

WHAT IF THE MESSAGE APPEARS TO COME FROM YOUR BOSS?

An employee receives an email that seems to come from the business owner.

It says:

"I'm in a meeting. Please process this transfer now. I'll explain later."

The employee may worry that refusing to act could create a problem.

A secure business should make the correct response straightforward.

Employees must know that unusual transfers and changes to payment instructions require the agreed checks, regardless of who appears to be requesting them.

Business owners and managers must follow those same rules.

For example, the business can establish that certain payments require a second authorised person to confirm the details before the transfer is completed.

The approval process should be practical for the size of the business, but it should not disappear simply because an email claims urgency.

WHAT SHOULD YOU DO IF YOU SUSPECT AN ATTEMPT?

If you have not sent money or information

  1. Stop the requested transaction or disclosure.
  2. Contact the supposed sender using previously trusted contact information.
  3. Inform the business owner, manager or designated security contact.
  4. Preserve the email and relevant conversation details.
  5. If your own mailbox may be affected, contact the email administrator or provider and begin securing the account.

Do not keep interacting with the suspicious sender to see what happens.

Avoid forwarding potentially harmful attachments unnecessarily.

If money has already been transferred

Contact your bank immediately using its official channels.

Explain that you suspect a fraudulent transfer and provide the transaction details.

Ask what urgent recall, restriction, investigation or recovery procedures may be available.

Where appropriate, contact the receiving financial institution through verified channels and follow the originating bank's instructions.

Then:

  • Inform the business owner or authorised management.
  • Contact the legitimate supplier or customer independently.
  • Preserve invoices, emails, payment references and related records.
  • Report the suspected fraud to the relevant authorities.
  • Review any affected business accounts and payment procedures.

In Nigeria, suspected cyber-enabled fraud can be reported to the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre through its official reporting portal:

https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

There is no guarantee that money will be recovered, even when the incident is reported promptly.

If your email account may have been compromised

Take appropriate steps with the account administrator or service provider:

  1. Secure the account and replace any compromised password.
  2. Review and revoke unfamiliar active sessions.
  3. Check multi-factor authentication and account recovery settings.
  4. Review mailbox forwarding rules, filters, delegated access and connected applications.
  5. Investigate suspicious sign-in activity and sent or deleted messages.
  6. Identify whether fraudulent emails were sent to customers or suppliers.
  7. Notify affected contacts through trusted channels where necessary.

If sensitive information may have been exposed, assess any applicable data protection obligations.

For complex incidents, seek qualified technical assistance.

ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO

The email that nearly delayed an entire delivery

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

Emeka ran a small electronics shop in Enugu. He had been discussing a new stock order with his regular supplier in Lagos for almost a week.

The supplier had confirmed the items, sent an invoice and promised to arrange dispatch once payment was received.

On Tuesday morning, Emeka opened the email conversation to arrange the transfer. A new message had appeared in the same thread, asking him to pay into a different bank account because the supplier was experiencing problems with its usual account.

The invoice number and amount were correct. Even the wording sounded like the supplier's usual messages.

Emeka asked his assistant to prepare the payment, but she pointed out that they had never used the new account before.

They tried calling the supplier's usual office number. Nobody answered.

By lunchtime, Emeka was getting frustrated. A customer had already paid a deposit for some of the accessories, and delaying the order could affect the delivery he had promised.

He considered proceeding with the transfer, but decided to keep it pending until someone at the supplier's office confirmed the change.

Later that afternoon, he reached his usual contact, who said the supplier had not authorised any new payment details.

Emeka still did not know whether someone had accessed the supplier's email account or was impersonating its correspondence. The supplier needed to investigate.

The order was delayed, and Emeka had an uncomfortable conversation with his customer about the revised delivery date.

But the business had not sent money to an unverified account.

What this story teaches: A fraudulent instruction can arrive inside a genuine-looking business conversation. Independent verification may cause inconvenience, but that is preferable to transferring money to an account that has not been confirmed.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

The invoice verification rule

Before acting on an unexpected payment instruction, staff should answer these questions:

  • Is this a new beneficiary or a change to existing payment details?
  • Have I checked the instruction through a contact method we trusted before this email arrived?
  • Did I confirm the change with an authorised person?
  • Does the request follow our business's normal approval process?
  • Is someone pressuring me to bypass those checks?
  • Have I informed the appropriate person if anything remains unclear?
  • Is the payment still on hold if the details have not been verified?

START HERE

Tell every employee who handles invoices or transfers: any unexpected change to bank details must be independently confirmed before payment.

Write down the rule, identify who performs the verification and make sure the business owner follows it too.
