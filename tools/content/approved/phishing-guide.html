Phishing: How Nigerian SMEs Are Targeted and What to Do About It

Four suspicious messages your business could receive, how to examine them and a simple way to teach your team what to do.

Explore four examples What to do after phishing

Four messages that look like ordinary business. Which would your team trust?

Your supplier sends updated payment details. Your manager needs an urgent transfer. A customer shares a document that requires you to sign in. Someone claiming to be from customer support asks you to read out a verification code.

Any of these situations could happen during a normal working day. Some requests will be genuine. Others may be attempts to steal information, take over an account or redirect money.

Phishing works by making a dangerous action look like a familiar task.

This guide takes you through four common situations, explains what deserves closer attention and shows how to make safer decisions. You can also use the examples to start a short conversation with employees.

Rule to remember

DON'T JUDGE A MESSAGE BY HOW REAL IT LOOKS. CHECK WHAT IT WANTS YOU TO DO.

Question the action
Verify independently

Start with the request, not the appearance

A phishing message may use a familiar name, correct business details and convincing language. It might even arrive through a real account that someone else has gained access to.

That is why poor spelling, unfamiliar logos and strange email addresses are not the only things to look for.

Pay particular attention when a message asks you to:

  • Enter a password or verification code.
  • Send money or change payment instructions.
  • Open an unexpected file or sign in through a link.
  • Share confidential business or customer information.
  • Ignore the normal approval process because something is urgent.

These requests are not automatically fraudulent. They are actions that deserve independent verification.

Fictional business example

Example 1: The supplier with new bank details

What to question
Unexpected payment-detail changeThe same invoice may be familiar
Independent check

Call the supplier using a trusted number

The situation

You receive a message from a supplier you have used for several months.

It refers to your latest order and states that the supplier's previous bank account is no longer in use.

The message asks you to pay the outstanding invoice into a new account.

What makes it convincing?

The sender appears familiar. The order is real, the invoice amount is correct and the message follows a conversation you recognise.

A criminal may have copied previous correspondence, impersonated the supplier or gained access to an existing email account.

What should you question?

The request changes something important about how your business sends money.

Even when the message is polite and accurate, it does not independently establish that the new banking details belong to your supplier.

The safer response

Call the supplier using a telephone number you already trust.

Confirm whether the account details have changed and whether the person requesting the change is authorised to do so.

Do not rely on a new contact number provided in the same message.

Keep the payment pending until the change is verified.

What your team should remember: A familiar conversation does not automatically make new payment instructions genuine.

Fictional business example

Example 2: The manager who cannot take calls

What to question
Pressure to bypass approvalThe sender claims to be senior
Independent check

Follow the established payment procedure

The situation

An employee receives a WhatsApp or email message appearing to come from the business owner.

It says an urgent business expense must be paid immediately. The sender explains that they are in a meeting and cannot answer calls.

The employee is told to make the transfer first and discuss the details later.

What makes it convincing?

The message appears to come from someone with authority.

It may use their name, photograph, title or usual style of communication. The request may sound like a genuine work assignment.

What should you question?

The sender is asking the employee to bypass normal payment approval.

The pressure to act quickly makes it harder to check whether the instruction is genuine.

The safer response

Follow the business's payment approval procedure.

Contact the manager using an established number or another agreed verification method. If that person is unavailable, wait for the required approval or use the business's authorised escalation process.

Employees should know that they are allowed to question unusual payment instructions, even when they appear to come from the owner.

What your team should remember: Seniority does not remove the need for payment verification.

Fictional business example

Example 3: The shared document that asks you to sign in

What to question
Sign-in requested through a linkProfessional appearance is not proof
Independent check

Open the service through the usual trusted route

The situation

An email arrives with a notification about a document supposedly shared by a customer or colleague.

You open the notification and are asked to sign in to Microsoft 365, Google or another familiar service.

The page looks professional and uses branding you recognise.

What makes it convincing?

Sharing documents is a normal part of business communication.

A fraudulent page can copy the appearance of a legitimate sign-in screen. Some attacks also misuse genuine authentication processes, so a familiar-looking page is not enough to establish that the request is safe.

What should you question?

Were you expecting the document? Does the sender normally share files this way? Why are you being asked to sign in now?

A website using HTTPS or displaying a padlock is not automatically trustworthy.

The safer response

Do not enter your password through an unexpected link.

Open the document-sharing service directly through your usual application or saved website address. Check whether the document appears there.

If you were not expecting anything, confirm the request with the sender through a separate, trusted channel.

A password manager can help identify some lookalike login websites, but it should not be your only safeguard.

What your team should remember: Open important services through the usual trusted route, not through an unexpected message.

Fictional business example

Example 4: The caller who needs your verification code

What to question
Someone requests a security codeThe genuine code does not validate the caller
Independent check

Do not share the code

The situation

Someone calls claiming to work for a bank, messaging service or customer-support team.

They explain that there is a problem with your business account.

A verification code arrives on your phone, and the caller asks you to read it aloud so they can resolve the issue.

What makes it convincing?

The caller may already know your name, business details or the service you use.

The verification code arrives as a genuine notification from the service.

What should you question?

The code may be intended to authorise a login, account recovery or another sensitive action initiated by someone else.

A genuine code arriving on your phone does not prove the caller is authorised to request it.

The safer response

Do not share the code.

End the call and contact the service through its official app, website or a previously verified support number.

If you have already shared a code, secure the affected account promptly and contact the provider for assistance.

What your team should remember: Never disclose a one-time password or account verification code to someone who unexpectedly requests it.

How to examine a suspicious message

You can teach staff to examine a message using four questions.

  1. Who is asking?
  2. Why now?
  3. What action is requested?
  4. How can I confirm it independently?

Who is asking?

Check the sender's address or number where available.

An unfamiliar address, unexpected contact or small change in a domain name may be a warning sign.

However, even a genuine account can be compromised. The sender's identity alone does not settle the question.

Why am I receiving this now?

Think about the timing and context.

Were you expecting the invoice, document, delivery notification or account request?

A convincing story may still contain an unexpected instruction.

What action does the message want?

This is often the most important question.

Is it asking you to sign in, approve a payment, reveal information, install something or skip a normal procedure?

The greater the potential consequence, the more important independent verification becomes.

How can I confirm it without using the message?

Use a number, application or website that you already know is genuine.

If you cannot confirm the request, leave the transaction or task pending and ask the appropriate person for assistance.

You do not need to prove that a message is malicious before deciding not to act on it.

A message can be genuine and still need verification

It is important to avoid treating every unusual request as proof of dishonesty.

A supplier may legitimately change banks. A customer may send an unexpected document. A manager may genuinely need an urgent payment.

The goal is not to accuse people. It is to confirm sensitive instructions before acting.

A good verification procedure protects both the business and the legitimate person making the request.

What if an employee has already responded?

The response depends on what happened.

What happened Immediate direction
They clicked a link but entered nothing Stop interacting with the page, report the concern and check for unusual activity.
They entered a password or shared a code Secure the affected account through its official service, review sessions and contact the provider or IT support.
They opened a suspicious file or installed software Stop using the device for sensitive work, notify technical support and arrange an appropriate security check.
They transferred money Contact the bank immediately, preserve transaction records and report suspected fraud to the appropriate authorities.

For detailed steps, use the response section of our Phishing article.

Do not punish staff for reporting a mistake. Early reporting gives the business a better chance to contain the problem and warn other employees.

A 10-minute phishing exercise for your team

This guide is most useful when employees discuss how they would respond to the examples.

You can do this during a normal team meeting. No special software or formal cybersecurity training session is required.

  1. 2 minutesExplain the rule
  2. 4 minutesDiscuss two examples
  3. 2 minutesAgree on reporting
  4. 2 minutesConfirm the rule

First two minutes: Explain the rule

Tell staff that unfamiliar or unexpected requests involving money, passwords, verification codes or sensitive information must be verified independently.

Next four minutes: Discuss two examples

Choose two situations from this article that relate to your business.

For a retail shop, you might use the changed supplier bank details and urgent manager payment requests.

For a small office, you might use the shared document and verification-code examples.

Ask employees to identify what action is being requested and how they would confirm it.

Next two minutes: Agree on the reporting process

Make sure everyone knows who to contact when a suspicious message arrives.

For a very small business, this could be the owner or manager. For a larger team, it may be an IT administrator or designated employee.

Final two minutes: Confirm the operating rule

Agree that no one should share a login code, change supplier payment details or approve an unusual transfer simply because a message appears genuine.

Employees should also know they can report a mistake promptly without fear of being blamed.

Repeat this short discussion periodically using examples relevant to your work.

Try these three decisions

Situation A

An established supplier emails an invoice with a different account number.

What should the employee do?

Recommended decision: Contact the supplier through an independently verified number, confirm the change and obtain the required approval before paying.

Situation B

A colleague sends an unexpected document link and asks you to sign in.

What should the employee do?

Recommended decision: Confirm that the document was actually shared and open the service through the usual trusted application or website.

Situation C

A caller claiming to be from customer support asks for the verification code that just arrived.

What should the employee do?

Recommended decision: Do not share the code. End the call and contact the service through an official support channel.

These exercises are designed to build decision-making habits, not to test whether employees can identify a message by its appearance alone.

Turn phishing awareness into a business habit

Pick the two examples in this guide that are most relevant to your business.

Discuss them with the people who handle emails, customer messages or payments.

Agree on three things: which requests require verification, how to verify them and whom staff should notify when something looks suspicious.

Start here

At your next staff meeting, ask everyone how they would handle an unexpected supplier bank-detail change. Agree on one verification rule and make sure the whole team knows it.
