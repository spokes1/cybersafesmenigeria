Password Security

One leaked password can put more than one account at risk.

Check your habits Why it matters
The big idea

One important account = one unique password

If you use the same password for your email, social media and business accounts, one compromised password could put several parts of your business at risk. The safer approach is simple: use a different password for every important account.

You do not need to memorise dozens of complicated passwords. A password manager can help you create and store them securely.

What is password security?

Password security means protecting your accounts with passwords that are difficult to guess and are not reused across different services.

Your email account deserves particular attention. It may be used to reset passwords for other accounts, receive security notifications and access important business information.

Think about the accounts your business depends on: email, banking, social media, cloud storage, payment services and business software. If someone gains access to one of them, the consequences could go beyond a single login.

Why should my business care?

Being a small business does not make you invisible to attackers. A compromised account could allow someone to:

  • Read private business emails or customer conversations.
  • Impersonate you when contacting customers or suppliers.
  • Attempt to change payment details or redirect invoices.
  • Access files stored in cloud services.
  • Reset passwords for other accounts.
  • Misuse a business social media page.

Attackers may also try passwords exposed in data breaches against other services. This is known as credential stuffing. For example, if a password leaked from an online shopping account is also used for your business email, an attacker may try that same combination elsewhere.

One leak, multiple risks

Password exposed on one service
Attacker tries the same password elsewhere
Email, social media or business accounts may be at risk

Password reuse allows one exposed password to create several account risks. This is a possible attack path, not a guaranteed outcome.

How do attackers get access?

Password reuse

A password exposed in one breach is tried on other websites and services.

Phishing

A fake email, text message or website tricks you into entering your login details.

Guessing

Weak or predictable passwords are easier to guess, especially when they contain publicly known information.

Malicious software

Some malware can steal saved credentials or capture information entered on an infected device.

Unauthorised access

Someone who knows a shared password may continue accessing an account even after their role in the business has changed.

You cannot control every attack, but you can make it much harder for someone to use a stolen or guessed password.

How can I tell if an account may be compromised?

Watch for signs such as:

  • A login notification from a device or location you do not recognise.
  • Password-reset messages you did not request.
  • Your password suddenly stops working.
  • Your recovery email address or phone number has changed without your permission.
  • Messages appear to have been sent from your account when you did not send them.
  • Unusual activity appears in your account or connected services.

Unknown login

An unfamiliar device has accessed your account.

Unexpected reset

You receive a password-reset notification you did not request.

Changed recovery details

Your recovery email or phone number has changed.

Unusual messages

Your account sends messages you do not recognise.

One warning sign does not always prove that an account has been hacked. However, unexpected activity deserves prompt investigation.

What should I do if I suspect someone has access?

Act quickly, starting with the affected account.

  1. Use a trusted device. If you suspect your device is infected, use another device you trust to secure the account.
  2. Change the affected password. If you reused it elsewhere, change those passwords too. Start with your email and other accounts that can reset passwords.
  3. Enable two-factor authentication (2FA). Use it wherever available. An authenticator app or security key can provide an additional option where supported.
  4. Review active sessions. Sign out devices or sessions you do not recognise.
  5. Check recovery settings. Confirm that the recovery email address and phone number are still yours.
  6. Review recent activity. Check for unfamiliar messages, transactions, forwarding rules or account changes.
  7. Contact the service provider. If you are locked out or notice financial activity you did not authorise, contact the provider or your bank through its official channels.

If the account belongs to your business, notify the appropriate person internally so other staff can watch for suspicious messages or requests.

A realistic business scenario

Imagine a small business owner uses one password for a personal account and the company's email. The password is exposed in a breach affecting the personal service.

Password exposed
Same password reused
Business email targeted
Possible account misuse

Using unique passwords reduces the risk of one exposed password being reused against other accounts.

An attacker tries the same login details against the business email. If the password still works and no additional protection stops the attempt, the attacker may gain access to business conversations, invoices or customer information. They could then use that access to send convincing messages in the owner's name.

The lesson: A password that works on several services creates several points of risk. Unique passwords help prevent a breach on one service from opening the door to another.

This is an illustrative scenario, not a report of a specific Nigerian incident.
