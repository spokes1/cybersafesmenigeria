Fake Bank Alerts

How to recognise fake transfer confirmations, verify payments and protect your business from losing goods or money.

The three-step payment check

The customer says the transfer was successful. Your account says otherwise.

A customer walks into your shop, selects a few items and asks to pay by bank transfer. Moments later, they show you a successful transaction receipt on their phone. You check your messages, but there is no credit alert yet.

The customer insists the money has left their account. They suggest it could be a network delay and ask you to release the goods.

Sometimes a genuine transfer is delayed. Other times, the payment confirmation is false. Knowing how to verify a transfer protects your business without unfairly accusing a customer.

Rule to remember

NO CONFIRMED CREDIT, NO RELEASE OF GOODS OR CASH.

A screenshot, SMS or transfer receipt is not enough. Confirm the payment independently through your own bank or payment provider before completing the transaction.

What is a fake bank alert?

A fake bank alert is a fraudulent payment notification designed to make someone believe money has been transferred when the expected payment has not been received.

It may appear as an SMS, an email, a screenshot or a payment receipt displayed on a customer's phone. Some fake alerts imitate genuine banking notifications. Others rely on a real-looking receipt that does not establish that the intended account was credited.

The aim is to persuade a seller to release goods, provide a service or hand over cash without receiving the agreed payment.

A successful-looking receipt is not the same as a confirmed payment.

A genuine transfer can also experience a delay. Missing credit does not automatically mean a customer is attempting fraud. It means the sale should remain pending while the business checks.

Why should your business care?

  • You could lose stock without receiving payment. Releasing goods against a false alert may mean losing the items and the expected revenue.
  • It can happen during an ordinary sale. An attacker may not need to break into your bank account. They may only need to persuade someone to accept a misleading receipt.
  • Busy periods make mistakes more likely. Staff at a crowded counter or dispatch point can feel pressured to complete transactions quickly.
  • A convincing receipt can create false confidence. A bank logo or transaction reference on a screenshot does not prove the business account has been credited.
  • The consequences can continue after the sale. Missing stock, disrupted orders and investigations cost time as well as money.

How does fake bank alert fraud happen?

  1. The customer requests a bank transfer. They choose goods or request a service and obtain the business's account details.
  2. They present apparent proof of payment. This may be a screenshot, SMS or transaction receipt.
  3. They encourage the seller to proceed. They may say the network is slow, insist that their account has been debited or claim to be in a hurry.
  4. The goods or service are released. The business accepts the receipt without checking its own payment records.
  5. The missing payment is noticed later. The expected credit cannot be found when the business reconciles its transactions.

Not every case follows this pattern. The common mistake is relying on the customer's evidence instead of independently checking the receiving account.

Warning signs to watch for

These signs are reasons to verify carefully, not proof that a customer is dishonest.

  • The customer insists that a screenshot is enough and discourages you from checking your account.
  • They apply unusual pressure to hurry or become impatient when you request confirmation.
  • There is no matching incoming credit in your official banking app, account history or merchant dashboard.
  • The amount or receiving account shown on the receipt does not match the sale.
  • The date or transaction details appear inconsistent with the purchase.
  • The customer asks you to release the goods based on an unconfirmed network-delay explanation.

A receipt can look convincing and still be false. Its appearance is not a substitute for independent payment verification.

Before you release the goods: a three-step payment check

  1. Step 1: Check your own account.

    Open your official banking app, account history or merchant dashboard yourself. Never use a link supplied by the customer to verify payment.

  2. Step 2: Match the transaction.

    Find a corresponding incoming credit recorded as received. Match the amount, time and other available details to the sale. Check the balance where useful, but do not confuse a different customer's payment with this transaction.

  3. Step 3: Hold the sale if payment is unconfirmed.

    Explain politely that the goods or service can only be released when the payment is confirmed. Record the pending order if your business uses order records.

If the customer says their account has been debited, ask them to contact their bank or payment provider. You can also contact your own bank through an official channel. A delay may be genuine, but it is not payment confirmation.

What you can say to the customer:

“Thank you. I can see your transfer receipt, but the payment has not reflected in our account yet. We'll complete the sale once our bank confirms it.”

What if you have already released the goods?

If you discover that the expected payment was never received, act promptly and keep accurate records.

  1. Confirm that the payment is missing. Recheck your transaction history and contact your bank or payment provider if necessary. Rule out a delayed or incorrectly matched credit before concluding fraud occurred.
  2. Inform the business owner or responsible manager. Record what was sold, when the goods were released and who handled the transaction.
  3. Preserve available evidence. Keep the receipt or screenshot, sales and delivery records, messages, customer contact information and relevant CCTV footage. Preserve original files where possible.
  4. Contact the customer through existing details if appropriate. Explain that payment has not been confirmed and request clarification. Do not threaten or publicly accuse a person based on an unverified transaction.
  5. Report suspected fraud. If there is evidence of deliberate deception, report the matter to the police. Nigeria's National Cybercrime Centre provides an online reporting portal at https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/.
  6. Improve the payment process. Identify why the transaction was approved and ensure staff can follow the confirmation procedure next time.
Important distinction: If there was no genuine transfer, there may be no bank transaction to reverse. The loss may be the goods or services obtained through deception.
If money has actually left your business account without authorisation: Contact your bank's fraud team immediately through an official channel. Ask it to secure the account and investigate available tracing or recovery options. Save transaction references and report the incident to the appropriate authorities. Recovery is not guaranteed.

How can you reduce the risk?

Make verification routine. Apply the same rule whether the customer is new, familiar, polite or in a hurry.

Give staff clear responsibility. Decide who checks incoming payments and who may approve the release of goods. Staff should not be penalised for following the verification procedure.

Use official merchant tools where available. Check incoming payments through a trusted bank or payment-provider dashboard instead of forwarded receipts.

Keep simple sales records. Record the amount, approximate time, order number and confirmation status, especially when multiple customers pay similar amounts.

Plan for network problems. If your usual app is unavailable, use another official verification channel if one is available. If payment still cannot be confirmed, keep the sale pending or agree on another acceptable payment method.

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

It was getting close to closing time at a phone-accessories shop in Lagos when Tunde handed a customer a wireless headset and a power bank to inspect. The customer agreed to pay by bank transfer and, a moment later, showed him a successful-payment screen.

Tunde checked the shop's banking app. No matching credit had appeared. The customer insisted the transfer was complete and pointed out that their ride was already waiting outside.

For a moment, Tunde was tempted to release the items. The shop had been busy, and he did not want another argument at the counter. But if he handed them over without confirmation, he would have no way of knowing whether the money would actually arrive.

He explained that he could keep the items aside while they checked the payment. The customer was unhappy and said they might return later. Tunde recorded the order as pending rather than accusing the customer of lying.

When the owner returned, they reviewed the available transaction records. They still could not establish whether the payment had simply been delayed or the receipt was misleading.

The sale remained unfinished. That was frustrating, but the shop had not released goods against an unconfirmed payment.

What this story teaches: A payment screenshot is a reason to check, not proof that money has reached the business. Staff need a fair way to hold a sale without accusing customers of fraud.

The payment rule to keep at your counter

  1. Check your own official banking channel.
  2. Match a confirmed incoming credit to the sale.
  3. If there is no confirmed credit, keep the sale pending.
Start here: Tell every employee who handles payments that a customer receipt is not the final approval to release goods. Show them how to confirm a transfer in the business account today.
