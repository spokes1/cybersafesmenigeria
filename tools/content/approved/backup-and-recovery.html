Backup & Recovery

The habit that decides whether a bad day is a setback or a major business disruption.

Check your backup habits Why it matters

Your business data is only one accident away from being lost.

Imagine opening your shop tomorrow and discovering that your laptop won't start. Your customer contacts, sales records, invoices and important business documents are all on that laptop.

Or imagine that ransomware locks your files and demands money before you can access them again.

Would you know where to find another copy?

Backup and recovery helps you prepare for situations like these. By keeping safe copies of important business information and knowing how to restore them, you can reduce downtime and avoid rebuilding everything from scratch.

What is backup and recovery?

A backup is a separate copy of your important data. Recovery is the process of getting that data back when the original is lost, damaged, deleted or made inaccessible.

For a Nigerian SME, important data might include:

Customer information

Customer contacts and order histories.

Money records

Sales records, invoices and payment records.

Business documents

Business documents and contracts.

Stock and suppliers

Product lists, inventory records and supplier details.

People and operations

Employee information and operational files.

You don't necessarily need an expensive system to get started. Depending on your business, a reputable cloud backup service, an external drive or a combination of both may be suitable.

Cloud backup service

Configure account permissions, access protection, retention and versioning.

External drive

Disconnect it when the backup is complete and it is no longer needed.

A combination of both

Keep copies somewhere that won't be affected by the same incident as your original files.

Why should your business care?

Data loss doesn't always begin with a cyberattack. Sometimes it's a damaged phone, a failed hard drive or an employee accidentally deleting an important folder. Whatever the cause, losing access to business records can create serious problems.

You could lose track of money

Without invoices, receipts and sales records, it becomes harder to confirm payments, follow up on outstanding balances and reconcile transactions.

You could lose customer trust

If customer orders and contact details disappear, fulfilling orders and answering enquiries can become difficult.

Your business could stop operating normally

A business that depends on a particular computer or employee to access essential records may struggle when that device or person becomes unavailable.

Ransomware could lock you out of your own files

Some attacks encrypt business data and demand payment for its supposed return. Having a separate, usable backup can give you another recovery option.

Recovery can take longer than expected

A backup that is incomplete, outdated or impossible to restore may not help when you need it most.

How does data loss happen?

Four common scenarios

Device loss or damage

A phone gets stolen, a laptop fails or liquid damages a device containing important records.

Accidental deletion

Someone deletes a folder, overwrites a document or removes information that the business still needs.

Ransomware

Malicious software encrypts files, preventing you from opening them. If the backup is connected to the same environment, it may also be affected.

Cloud account or synchronisation problems

Files can be deleted, overwritten or affected by compromised accounts. Synchronisation is useful, but changes or deletions may synchronise too. It should not automatically be treated as a complete backup strategy.

In each situation, the underlying question is the same: Can you recover a clean, usable copy of the information?

How do you know your backup arrangements need attention?

Check for these warning signs:

  • You don't know where your customer records would be if your main laptop or phone disappeared today.
  • Your only extra copy is another folder on the same device.
  • Your external drive stays connected to the computer all the time.
  • You assume your files are backed up but have never checked the backup status.
  • Nobody has tried restoring a file to confirm the backup works.
  • Important records exist only on one employee's device, with no agreed recovery process.
  • Your business relies on cloud storage but has no plan for recovering deleted, corrupted or inaccessible files.
You don't have to wait until something goes wrong to discover whether your backup works.

A good backup is regular, separate, protected and tested.

  1. Identify the files you cannot afford to loseStart with the records your business needs to keep operating: customer details, financial records, orders, supplier information and essential documents.
    Prioritise the information that would be difficult or expensive to recreate.
  2. Set up regular backupsChoose a backup schedule based on how often your data changes. A business that processes orders throughout the day may need more frequent backups than one that updates a few documents each week.
    Where possible, automate the process so that it doesn't depend on someone remembering to do it.
  3. Keep a separate copyStore backup copies separately from your everyday working device.
    An external drive can be useful, but disconnect it when the backup is complete and it is no longer needed. If ransomware infects a computer while the drive is connected, the drive may also be exposed.
    Cloud backups can also help, but configure appropriate account permissions, access protection, retention and versioning. A separate backup account or service may offer additional protection when properly configured.
  4. Test your recovery processDon't stop at checking whether a backup job completed successfully.
    Try restoring a sample file and confirm that it opens correctly. Periodically test the recovery of more important information as well.
    A backup that has never been tested is an assumption, not a proven recovery plan.
  5. Decide who is responsibleEven in a small business, someone should be responsible for checking backup status, resolving failures and arranging recovery tests.
    Make sure another authorised person knows how to access essential records if the person responsible is unavailable.
  6. Protect access to your backupsUse strong, unique passwords and enable two-factor authentication where supported. Limit backup access to people who need it, and keep recovery information somewhere safe.
    A backup can only protect your business if an attacker cannot easily delete it or use it to gain access to other systems.

What should you do if something goes wrong?

If you suspect ransomware

  1. Disconnect the affected device from Wi-Fi and shared networks to help limit further spread. Don't connect backup drives to it.
  2. Contact a trusted IT or cybersecurity professional. Preserve relevant evidence and avoid actions that could make recovery more difficult.
  3. Don't rush to pay. Payment does not guarantee that your files will be restored. Get appropriate advice before making decisions.
  4. Check your backup options safely. Confirm that the copies are unaffected before attempting a restoration.
  5. Restore only when it is safe to do so. Make sure the cause of the incident has been addressed to reduce the risk of reinfection.
  6. Report the incident through appropriate channels. In Nigeria, you can consult the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre's official reporting portal at nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport.
Avoid immediately deleting files, reinstalling everything or restoring a backup onto a potentially compromised environment without getting suitable advice.

If a device is lost, stolen or damaged

  1. Check for a recent copy.Check your cloud storage and other backup locations for a recent copy of your files.
  2. Secure your accounts.If the device was stolen, secure relevant accounts from another trusted device, change exposed passwords and use available device-locking or remote-wipe features where appropriate.
  3. Restore what you need.Restore the information you need from a safe, verified backup.
  4. Learn from it.Review what was lost and improve your backup arrangements so you are better prepared next time.

An illustrative example: a small business loses its records

This is a fictional scenario illustrating a common business risk, not a report of a specific incident.

A small clothing business keeps customer orders, supplier contacts and sales records on one laptop. One morning, the laptop fails to start.

Laptop fails to start

At first, the owner assumes the files are gone. Fortunately, the business has automatic backups of its important records and a separate copy that is not permanently connected to the laptop.

After the device is repaired or replaced, the owner restores the files and checks that the records are usable. The business still experiences disruption, but it avoids having to recreate months of information manually.

The lesson: Having a backup is a good start. Knowing that it is recent, separate and recoverable is what makes it useful.
