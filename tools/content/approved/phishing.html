The message looks familiar. The request is what you should question.

You're busy attending to customers when a message arrives from someone claiming to be your supplier.

They've sent an updated invoice and need you to open a link to view it. The message uses the supplier's name, mentions an order you're expecting and asks you to act quickly.

Everything seems normal until the link opens a page asking for your business email password.

This is how phishing can enter an ordinary working day. It often looks like a familiar task: checking an invoice, confirming a delivery, updating an account or responding to a colleague.

The danger isn't always obvious. Understanding what the message is asking you to do gives you a better chance of recognising the problem before it affects your business.

Rule to remember

WHEN A MESSAGE ASKS YOU TO ACT, VERIFY THE REQUEST THROUGH A CHANNEL YOU TRUST.

Pause before acting
Use a trusted contact

What is phishing?

Phishing is a type of online fraud where someone uses a deceptive message to trick you into revealing information, opening a harmful link, installing software or taking an action that benefits the attacker.

The message may pretend to come from a bank, supplier, delivery company, government agency or someone within your own business.

Phishing can arrive through several channels:

  • Email: A false invoice, account warning or document-sharing notification.
  • SMS: A message asking you to confirm a transaction or update account details.
  • WhatsApp or social media: A suspicious link or unusual request from an unfamiliar or apparently familiar contact.
  • Phone calls: Someone pretending to be a bank representative, support agent or colleague who asks for confidential information.

Phishing through SMS is sometimes called smishing, while phishing through phone calls is known as vishing. The names are less important than recognising the deception.

Not every phishing attempt involves a suspicious website. Some simply ask the recipient to reply with confidential information, share a verification code or authorise a payment.

Why should your business care?

Your business email can become an entry point

If someone obtains your email password, they may try to access business conversations, customer information and other accounts connected to that address.

A fraudulent message can lead to financial loss

An attacker may impersonate a supplier or manager and persuade an employee to send money to the wrong account.

Customers may receive messages that appear to come from you

If a business email or messaging account is compromised, attackers may use it to contact customers and request payments or personal information.

One employee's response can affect other people

A harmful attachment or stolen account could expose business records and create problems for colleagues, suppliers or customers.

Small businesses often depend on a few important accounts

When your main email, banking access or customer messaging account is disrupted, everyday business activities can become difficult.

How does a phishing attempt work?

Most attempts rely on a familiar sequence.

  1. The attacker chooses a believable identity. They may pretend to be your bank, a supplier, a courier or someone you work with.
  2. A message creates a reason to act. It might mention an unpaid invoice, a delivery problem, an account restriction or a document requiring attention.
  3. You are directed towards a particular action. The message asks you to open a link, enter a password, share a code, download a file or follow new payment instructions.
  4. The attacker benefits if you comply. They may obtain account access, collect confidential information, install harmful software or redirect a payment.

The message doesn't need to contain spelling mistakes or strange graphics to be dangerous.

Some fraudulent messages are carefully written. Others may even arrive from a genuine account that has been compromised.

Warning signs to watch for

A message deserves extra attention when you notice one or more of these signs.

An unexpected request for confidential information

Someone asks for your password, banking PIN, one-time password (OTP) or account recovery code.

An OTP is a temporary code used to verify a login or transaction. Never share it in response to an unexpected message or call.

Pressure to act immediately

The sender claims your account will be suspended, an order cancelled or a payment delayed unless you respond at once.

A link to an unfamiliar website

The message asks you to sign in through a page you do not recognise, even though it uses familiar colours or logos.

The main website address matters. A page can display a padlock or use HTTPS and still be fraudulent.

An unusual sender or contact detail

The email address or phone number is different from the one you normally use for that organisation.

However, a familiar sender is not proof of safety. An attacker may use a compromised account.

An unexpected attachment or document

You receive a file you were not expecting, supposedly containing an invoice, waybill, account statement or business document.

A payment instruction that changes unexpectedly

A supplier or colleague suddenly asks for money to be sent to a new account or insists that the usual approval process should be skipped.

A single warning sign doesn't always mean a message is fraudulent. It means you should verify the request before proceeding.

What should you do when you receive a suspicious message?

The message and the independent check

The risky path

  1. 1Unexpected message
  2. 2Open its link
  3. 3Enter information or follow instructions

The safer path

  1. 1Unexpected message
  2. 2Pause
  3. 3Contact the organisation independently
  4. 4Act only after verification

You can usually protect yourself by changing how you respond to the request.

Stop and examine what is being requested

Consider whether you were expecting the message. Is the sender asking you to do something unusual, disclose confidential information or bypass a normal business procedure?

Avoid using the message to verify itself

Do not call a phone number supplied only in the suspicious message or use its link to confirm that the sender is genuine.

Instead, contact the organisation through an established phone number, official app or website you already trust.

Open important services directly

If a message claims there is a problem with your bank account or business email, open the service through your usual app, saved bookmark or independently entered website address.

Do not sign in through a link just because the page looks genuine.

Confirm unusual requests with the person involved

If a manager asks for an urgent transfer or a supplier changes payment details, contact them independently before taking action.

A separate phone call using a previously verified number can prevent a costly mistake.

Report the message within your business

Tell the person responsible for IT or business operations. If several employees use the same email system, others may have received a similar message.

Use your email service's phishing-reporting feature where available.

Make it easy for staff to raise concerns. Reporting a suspicious message should be treated as a useful security action, not a reason for blame.

Already clicked or shared something? Here's what to do.

The right response depends on what happened.

What happened? Choose the relevant response.
  • Clicked only

    Stop interacting, stay alert and report concerns.

  • Shared a password or code

    Secure the account and notify support.

  • Opened a harmful file

    Stop sensitive activity and arrange a device check.

  • Sent money or banking details

    Contact the bank immediately and preserve evidence.

You clicked a link but entered nothing

Close the suspicious page and do not interact with it further.

If you did not enter information, download files or install software, the risk may be limited. Clicking alone does not prove that your account or device has been compromised.

Nevertheless, remain alert for unusual activity. If the link was opened on a work device, inform the person responsible for IT or security. Consider running a scan using updated, trusted security software.

You entered a password or shared a login code

Act promptly.

  1. Open the genuine service directly using a trusted device or app.
  2. Change the affected password immediately.
  3. Change passwords on other accounts where the same password was used.
  4. Sign out unfamiliar sessions and review account activity where the service provides those options.
  5. Enable or review multi-factor authentication, which adds another check during sign-in.
  6. If you approved an unfamiliar login request or shared a verification code, notify the service provider or your IT support team so they can investigate unauthorised access.

For business email accounts, an administrator may also need to check recovery details, forwarding rules and connected applications.

Do not assume that changing the password alone has resolved every form of account compromise.

You downloaded a file or installed software

Stop opening the suspicious file or following further instructions.

Avoid using the affected device for banking or sensitive business activities until it has been checked.

Run an updated security scan where appropriate. If you suspect harmful software is running, disconnect the affected device from the network and seek qualified technical assistance.

Tell your IT provider or the person responsible for the business's devices what happened. Preserve relevant evidence, including the original message, and avoid wiping the device without considering investigation and recovery needs.

You shared banking details or sent money

Contact your bank or payment provider immediately using an official support channel.

Explain what happened and ask about available account-protection, investigation and recovery measures.

Keep transaction references, the suspicious messages and other relevant records.

Where fraud or unauthorised activity is suspected, you can report it to the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre through its official reporting portal:

https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

You can also report a cybersecurity incident to the Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team through its official website:

https://cert.gov.ng/

Reporting promptly is important, but recovery of lost funds or information cannot be guaranteed.

How can a small business reduce phishing risks?

You don't need to expect every employee to identify every fraudulent message. A few consistent protections can make mistakes less damaging.

Secure the accounts that matter most

Use strong, unique passwords for your business email, banking and administrator accounts. Enable multi-factor authentication where available, preferably a phishing-resistant method such as a passkey or security key when supported.

Keep devices and applications updated

Regular updates and reputable security software can reduce the risk from harmful attachments, websites and known software weaknesses.

Set rules for payments and sensitive information

Require independent confirmation before changing supplier bank details, making unusual payments or sharing confidential customer information.

Teach staff one simple reporting habit

If something feels unusual, they should stop and ask before acting. If they have already clicked or shared information, they should report it quickly.

Limit unnecessary account access

Employees should only have access to the systems and information needed for their work. Remove access when it is no longer required.

These measures help the business respond more effectively when a deceptive message gets through.

The customer's artwork that asked for an email password

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

A printing business in Lagos received an urgent request from a customer who needed promotional flyers ready the following morning.

The customer sent a link to the artwork, explaining that the file was too large to attach directly.

Femi, who handled customer orders, opened the link. Instead of displaying the artwork, the page asked him to sign in using the business's email address and password.

He had used shared-document services before, so the request didn't seem completely unusual. But something made him hesitate. Why would a customer's artwork require access to the shop's email account?

The customer had already called twice about the deadline, and Femi didn't want to delay the job.

He considered entering the details just to retrieve the file.

Instead, he closed the page and contacted the customer through the number recorded with the order. He explained what had appeared and asked whether the file could be shared through their usual arrangement.

The customer said they hadn't expected an email-password request either.

Femi reported the incident to the shop owner. They couldn't immediately establish whether the link was malicious or whether it had been shared incorrectly.

But Femi hadn't entered the business's credentials.

The order was delayed while they found another way to receive the artwork.

What this story teaches: A request can relate to genuine business work and still require verification. Unexpected password prompts should never be trusted simply because someone is waiting for a job to be completed.

A suspicious message? Make these three decisions.

1. Was I expecting this request?

An unexpected message asking for account access, confidential information or money deserves verification.

2. Can I confirm it independently?

Use a phone number, official app or website that you trust, rather than the contact details supplied in the message.

3. Has anyone already acted on it?

If a password, code, file or payment was involved, follow the appropriate response steps immediately.

Tell everyone who handles business email or WhatsApp: an unexpected request for a password, verification code or payment must be verified through a separate, trusted channel.
