Something has gone wrong. What should you do first?

A customer calls to say someone using your business WhatsApp account has asked them for money.

At the same time, your sales assistant cannot access the account. They want to keep trying passwords. Another employee suggests posting a warning on Facebook. You are not sure which action should come first.

In a situation like this, the worst response is usually confusion: several people make changes, important details disappear, and nobody knows who is responsible for contacting customers or the service provider.

A cyber incident does not mean your business needs a large IT department. It does mean somebody needs to take charge, prevent further harm and record what happened.

Rule to remember: STOP FURTHER HARM. TELL THE RIGHT PERSON. KEEP THE EVIDENCE. THEN RECOVER SAFELY.

What is incident response?

Incident response is the way a business handles a suspected or confirmed security problem. It begins when someone notices something unusual and continues through containment, investigation, safe recovery and improving the business's procedures.

Examples include:

  • A suspicious transfer or an unexpected change to supplier payment details.
  • A WhatsApp Business, email or social media account that someone else may be using.
  • A laptop showing harmful-software warnings or business files becoming inaccessible.
  • A lost work phone containing customer conversations.
  • Customer records accidentally sent to the wrong person.

Not every warning is an attack. An unusual sign-in may belong to an authorised employee; an unavailable file may have an ordinary technical cause. You should still take sensible precautions while establishing what happened.

The aim is not to blame somebody or solve every technical question immediately. It is to make the next safe decision.

Money may have gone to the wrong account

Example: An employee has just sent a transfer after receiving unfamiliar supplier instructions.

  1. Contact your bank immediately through its official app, website or verified fraud-support number. Provide the transaction reference and ask what recall, restriction or investigation measures may be possible.
  2. Put any further payments connected to the request on hold. Independently contact the legitimate supplier using previously trusted details.
  3. Preserve the transfer receipt, original messages and relevant account records. Inform the business owner or authorised decision-maker.
  4. Report suspected cyber-enabled fraud through an appropriate Nigerian law-enforcement channel. See the reporting section below.

Do not wait for a complete investigation before contacting the bank. A request to recall funds is not a guarantee they will be recovered.

A business account may have been taken over

Example: Staff are locked out of WhatsApp Business or customers receive messages nobody authorised.

  1. From a separate trusted device, use the provider's official recovery process. Follow its instructions to protect sign-in details, recovery options and active sessions.
  2. Tell the owner or designated lead. Use another established channel to warn contacts only when there is a credible reason to believe they may receive fraudulent messages. Avoid posting unverified claims or exposing private details.
  3. Review account access, linked devices, forwarding rules and suspicious messages as supported by that service. Preserve alerts and useful records.
  4. If financial instructions or personal information were affected, follow the relevant payment and privacy response too.

Do not share verification codes with someone claiming they can recover the account for you. If you cannot regain access, use the provider's official support route.

A work computer may be infected or files are locked

Example: A laptop shows a malware warning, unfamiliar behaviour or a ransom demand.

  1. Stop using the device for sensitive tasks. Where safe and appropriate, disconnect its network connection to help limit further access or spread.
  2. Do not attach backup drives, install random cleaning programmes, delete evidence or restore files onto an unchecked device.
  3. Inform the designated lead and preserve the warning, approximate time and details of recent downloads or changes. Seek trusted technical help if needed.
  4. Decide on the correct recovery approach after assessing the incident. This may involve a professionally managed rebuild, checks on affected accounts and restoration from protected, verified backups.

Do not follow a blanket rule to switch every affected device off: shutting down or wiping a system can destroy useful evidence, while an unsafe system may need urgent isolation. Ask for qualified advice where the impact is significant.

Customer or staff information may have been exposed

Example: A customer spreadsheet was shared with the wrong WhatsApp group, or a laptop storing employee records is missing.

  1. Stop additional exposure where possible: revoke shared-link access, correct permissions or secure the relevant account or device.
  2. Record what information may be involved, who could have received it, when the issue was discovered and what has been done so far.
  3. Inform the business owner or person responsible for privacy matters and assess whether the incident creates risks to the people affected.
  4. Consider notification duties under Nigeria's data protection law and seek qualified advice where needed. Do not assume every mistake requires notification, or that simply deleting the original message removes every copy.

Important: If more than one category applies, act on each relevant risk. A stolen phone may involve account access, customer data and payments at the same time.

Who should do what?

A small business does not need a formal incident-response department, but employees should know who makes decisions.

The person who notices the problem should stop the risky activity, report it promptly and explain what they saw. They should not have to prove an attack occurred before raising the concern.

The owner or designated response lead should record the event, decide what needs immediate action, contact external parties and manage customer or staff updates. If the owner is unavailable, identify a backup decision-maker.

Someone responsible for accounts and payments should contact the bank through verified channels when money is at risk, and preserve the financial records.

A trusted technical or service-provider contact, where available, should investigate affected systems or assist with recovery. If the business does not already have an IT supplier, it can seek a qualified independent professional without treating the first unfamiliar caller as trustworthy.

One person may perform several of these roles. What matters is knowing who is responsible and avoiding conflicting actions.

First actions that help in nearly every incident

Stop the immediate harm

Put a suspicious transfer on hold. Stop entering passwords into a questionable website. Isolate a potentially infected device where safe. Revoke an accidentally shared link if you can.

Choose the action suited to what happened. Avoid random fixes that could make the incident harder to understand.

Make a short incident record

Write down the date and time the issue was noticed; who reported it; which devices, services or accounts may be affected; what unusual activity was observed; and what actions were taken.

Keep relevant emails, screenshots, transaction references or provider notifications. Limit who can access the evidence, especially where it contains personal information.

If you suspect serious criminal activity or a significant technical compromise, do not destroy records by unnecessarily resetting or wiping affected systems before considering investigation needs.

Communicate using a channel you trust

If the business email may be compromised, do not rely only on that email account to coordinate the response. If WhatsApp Business is affected, use an unaffected, established communication method.

Tell employees what they must temporarily stop doing and who may approve the next step. Where customers or suppliers face a credible risk of receiving fraudulent requests, issue a clear, factual warning from a verified channel.

Avoid speculation, public blame or promises that the situation is fully resolved before it has been checked.

Find out what was affected

Ask practical questions: Was money sent? Can people still access their accounts? Is the problem limited to one phone or computer? Are customer records involved? What is the next business activity that cannot safely continue?

Answers can change as the investigation develops. Keep updating the incident record rather than treating the first explanation as a confirmed cause.

What about the next few hours and days?

Protect essential business operations

If the affected system is unavailable, decide which tasks can continue safely using unaffected devices, reliable paper records or separate business accounts. Verify orders and payments independently rather than copying uncertain records into another system.

If customer deliveries or service commitments will be delayed, communicate clearly through a trusted channel. Do not expose sensitive information while explaining the disruption.

Investigate before restoring

A system that starts working again is not necessarily safe. A password reset may not remove an unfamiliar connected application; deleting a suspicious file may not address an underlying compromise.

Use trusted technical assistance when needed. Confirm that recovery accounts, devices and backup copies are appropriate before reconnecting services or restoring data.

Review legal, contractual and service-provider duties

Depending on the incident, the business may need to notify its bank, affected customers, a service provider, a regulator or law enforcement.

Under Section 40 of the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, a data controller must notify the Nigeria Data Protection Commission within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach likely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Where the breach is likely to create a high risk, affected individuals must also be informed immediately, subject to the Act's conditions.

This is not a requirement to report every suspicious login, lost device or accidental disclosure within 72 hours. The circumstances and legal role of the business matter. Seek appropriate advice promptly when personal data may have been breached.

Decide when normal work can resume

Before returning a device or account to normal use, check that the suspected problem has been addressed, relevant access has been secured, critical records are available and the person responsible has agreed that the service is safe enough to use.

Record what remains uncertain. Some incidents require continued monitoring, follow-up with banks or service providers, or further investigation.

Where can a Nigerian business report an incident?

A bank or payment provider: For suspected fraudulent payments, call its verified fraud or customer-support channel immediately. Keep the transfer details and a record of the report. Banking contact should not wait for a police report.

Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre (NPF-NCCC): For suspected cybercrime, impersonation, unauthorised access or cyber-enabled fraud, use the official reporting portal: https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team (ngCERT): For cybersecurity incidents and coordination, use its corporate incident-reporting channel: https://cert.gov.ng/incident-report/corporate . ngCERT also publishes the reporting information it requests, such as affected systems and an incident summary.

Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC): Where personal data may have been breached, review the applicable requirements and official guidance: https://ndpc.gov.ng/

The appropriate channel depends on the incident. Reporting does not guarantee an investigation outcome, immediate recovery or reimbursement.

Make your own incident-response card before you need it

You don't need a complicated document to get started. Write one page and keep it somewhere staff can access even if email or the main business computer is unavailable.

Include:

  • The person staff should contact first, plus a backup contact.
  • Your bank's verified fraud-support details and the names of people authorised to discuss business payments.
  • How to reach the people who manage your business email, website, phone numbers and other important accounts.
  • Which devices, customer records and business operations are most important.
  • Where protected backups are held, who may approve restoration and who can arrange trusted technical help.
  • Where staff should record incident times, messages and actions.
  • Which official reporting resources to use if needed.

Review the sheet after staff changes, service-provider changes or a real incident. Keep it secure because it may contain sensitive business information.

A realistic example: Three calls in one morning

A short illustrative example, not a report of a real incident.

At a small wholesale business, a customer calls to ask why the shop's WhatsApp account has requested a second payment. Minutes later, the attendant realises they can no longer sign in to that account.

The owner does not yet know whether someone has taken over the number, linked another device or is impersonating the shop from elsewhere. Rather than announcing that the account has been hacked, she uses the shop's established telephone line to contact the customer and confirm what message they received.

She tells staff to stop relying on the disputed WhatsApp conversation for payment instructions, begins the provider's official recovery process and saves the customer's screenshots. Orders may be delayed while the situation is investigated.

The lesson is not that every problem can be solved immediately. It is that the business can reduce further harm without knowing the full technical cause first.

When a cyber incident happens

A five-part reference for employees and business owners

    Pause:
