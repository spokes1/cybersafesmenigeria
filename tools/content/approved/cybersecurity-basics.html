You might run a shop with one smartphone, a small restaurant with a payment terminal, or an office where several employees use the same computer.

Maybe you do not have a website. You might not even describe your business as digital.

But customers send orders through WhatsApp. Suppliers email invoices. Money moves through banking apps. Your phone holds delivery addresses, and your computer may contain records you would struggle to recreate.

That means some of the things keeping your business running depend on technology.

You do not need to learn every cybersecurity term before you can protect them. You need to know what matters to your business, what could interrupt it and which sensible steps to take first.

RULE TO REMEMBER

PROTECT THE ACCOUNTS, MONEY, DEVICES AND INFORMATION YOUR BUSINESS DEPENDS ON. MAKE SURE EVERYONE KNOWS HOW TO ASK FOR HELP.

What is cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity means protecting your digital accounts, devices, systems and information against unauthorised access, misuse, disruption and other harm.

For a small business, this can mean stopping someone from taking over its WhatsApp account, checking that a transfer has actually arrived, keeping customer details away from unnecessary recipients or being able to recover orders after a laptop fails.

It is not just about hackers. Devices can break, employees can make mistakes, and important records can go missing without anyone attacking the business.

Good security helps reduce those risks, but it cannot guarantee that nothing will ever go wrong.

1. Accounts: Who can act as your business?

Think about business email, WhatsApp Business, social media, online banking, cloud storage and applications used to receive orders.

If someone takes control of an important account, they may read information, impersonate the business or interfere with its operations.

Start here: Identify the accounts the business cannot operate without. Check who can sign in, use a different strong password for each account and turn on multi-factor authentication where supported. Passkeys are a useful option when the service offers them.

Email deserves particular attention because it may be used to reset passwords for other accounts.

Learn more: Password Security and Account Security.

2. Payments: How do you know money is going to the right place?

A payment screenshot, a familiar WhatsApp name or an email from a regular supplier is not enough to establish that a transaction is genuine.

A business needs a clear way to confirm incoming transfers before releasing goods and to approve outgoing payments, especially when beneficiary details change.

Start here: Agree on who checks incoming credits and who approves outgoing transfers. Confirm unusual supplier instructions through contact details already trusted by the business, not details supplied in the disputed message.

A transfer that is still pending may need time to be resolved. Do not automatically repeat it without checking its status and obtaining the appropriate approval.

Learn more: Payment Security and Fake Bank Alerts.

3. Information: What would be difficult to replace or embarrassing to expose?

Your business may hold customer names, delivery addresses, order histories, staff information, invoices and supplier contacts.

Some records need to be available every day; others should only be seen by authorised people.

Start here: Choose one important set of records. Find out where it is kept, who has access and whether there is a protected backup that can actually be restored. Avoid unnecessary copies on personal devices and in chat groups.

If personal information is involved, the business also needs to understand its applicable data protection responsibilities.

Learn more: Data Protection and Backup & Recovery.

4. Devices: What would happen if this phone or computer stopped working?

A shared counter computer, business owner's phone, laptop and other connected equipment may all carry business information or provide access to important accounts.

Start here: Keep supported software updated, lock screens, review installed applications and limit who can make administrator-level changes. If an employee uses a personal device for authorised work, agree on suitable protection and access rules.

Do not assume that a device is safe merely because it looks normal. Many security problems are not immediately visible.

Learn more: Device Security and Mobile Security.

5. People and response: Who knows what to do when something feels wrong?

Even sensible security settings will not answer every question. An employee may receive an unusual payment request. Someone may lose a business phone. An account may show a sign-in alert nobody recognises.

Start here: Choose a person employees can contact about security concerns. Explain that unusual requests should be paused and verified, and that reporting a possible mistake promptly is better than hiding it.

The first action depends on the problem. For a suspicious transfer, contacting the bank urgently may matter most. For an infected device, safe isolation and technical assessment may be more appropriate.

SMALL STEPS, THIS WEEK

Your first week: five manageable improvements

You do not have to buy a complicated product or make every change in one afternoon. Begin with tasks that match how your business operates.

  1. 1

    First: Write down your essential accounts. Include the email address, social media profile, business WhatsApp and other services the business depends on. Record who is responsible for them and how recovery is managed. Store sensitive recovery details securely rather than putting passwords in an open notebook or group chat.

  2. 2

    Second: Protect your most important sign-ins. Fix reused passwords, enable supported multi-factor authentication or passkeys, and check whether former employees or contractors still have access.

  3. 3

    Third: Agree on a payment-verification rule. Tell everyone who handles orders or invoices exactly how to confirm incoming credits and changes to outgoing payment details. Make sure the owner follows the rule too.

  4. 4

    Fourth: Identify one critical record and its recovery plan. Work out how your business would retrieve orders, customer details or invoices if its main device failed. Arrange a protected backup and check that the files can be restored.

  5. 5

    Fifth: Give staff a reporting contact. Make it clear whom to tell about strange sign-in notices, suspicious messages, lost phones, accidental disclosures and unexpected software warnings.

These are starting points, not a complete cybersecurity programme. Your business may have additional risks, especially if it handles sensitive customer information or depends on specialised systems.

What do common cybersecurity terms actually mean?

Phishing:
A deceptive message or website intended to trick someone into handing over information, opening harmful content or taking another risky action.
Social engineering:
Using persuasion, impersonation, urgency or other manipulation to influence someone's decisions.
Malware:
Software intended to perform harmful or unauthorised activity on a device or system.
Ransomware:
A form of malware-related extortion that may lock data, steal information or disrupt operations while demanding payment.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA):
Using more than one factor to verify a sign-in. Depending on the service, this may involve an authenticator, security key or another supported method.
Passkey:
A sign-in method designed to resist many common phishing attempts and reduce reliance on remembered passwords.
Backup:
A protected copy of important information that can be used for recovery. A backup is only useful if it is available and can be restored when needed.
Account takeover:
When an unauthorised person gains control of an account such as email, WhatsApp or social media.
Data breach:
A security incident involving personal data, such as unauthorised disclosure, access, loss or alteration. Legal reporting duties depend on the circumstances.

Questions business owners often ask

Is cybersecurity only for businesses with websites?

No. If you use WhatsApp, email, online banking, digital payment tools or a computer for orders, some part of your business depends on digital systems. Start with what you actually use.

Do I need to hire an IT specialist immediately?

Not necessarily. Owners and employees can carry out many basic actions, such as enabling stronger sign-ins, verifying payments and agreeing on reporting rules. Some problems, including a suspected compromise, complex network setup or recovery from serious malware, may require qualified help.

Is antivirus enough to protect my business?

No single application prevents every problem. Device protection and updates help, but they do not replace safe payment procedures, account security, staff awareness or backups.

Should everyone in the business know the passwords?

No. Where supported, use separate accounts and give each person only the access needed for their work. Shared access, when unavoidable, needs careful management and should be reviewed when staff roles change.

What if we have already been affected by a scam or cyber incident?

Do not rely on a generic checklist alone. The right first step depends on what has happened. Contact your bank promptly for suspected financial fraud, use official recovery channels for account takeovers and seek trusted technical support for affected devices. Preserve relevant evidence and use the Incident Response guide for situation-specific steps.

Suspected cybercrime in Nigeria can also be reported through the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre's official e-reporting portal: https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/ .
