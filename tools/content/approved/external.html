01

You don't have to learn everything at once

You run a business. You may have a shop to open, customer orders to deliver and staff who depend on clear instructions. Finding time to study cybersecurity is not always easy.

This page brings together a small selection of free-to-read guidance from Nigerian organisations, international cybersecurity agencies and the companies behind everyday services such as WhatsApp.

You do not need to work through every resource. Choose one that matches the issue in front of you, find an action you can apply, and return when you need something more detailed.

Rule to remember: A useful guide should lead to a practical change in your business, not just another document saved on your phone.

02

Start with a learning path

01

1. I want to understand the basics

Begin with the NCC Cyber Security Awareness page or the UK NCSC Small Organisations Guide. Look for advice on securing accounts, protecting devices and identifying suspicious requests.

If you are the only person running the business, the NIST Small Business Cybersecurity: Non-Employer Firms guide offers a more structured way to think about risk. It was written in a US context, so treat its legal or institutional references separately from Nigerian requirements.

Suggested first action: Choose one important account or device and check its current protection rather than trying to change everything today.

02

2. I need something to discuss with employees

Use a short awareness guide from Microsoft Security or the WhatsApp Help Center. For a practical session, the UK NCSC's suspected phishing email exercise provides discussion material for approximately 15–30 minutes. You can adapt the questions to your staff's actual work, including customer payments, supplier messages and WhatsApp orders.

Suggested first action: Ask employees what they would do if someone requested a verification code or a change to supplier bank details. Agree on who should verify the request.

03

3. I want to understand a particular concern

For cybersecurity alerts relevant to Nigeria, consult ngCERT's published advisories. For handling personal data, start with the Nigeria Data Protection Commission's public guidance. For broader small-business planning, explore NIST's Small Business Cybersecurity Corner.

Suggested first action: Write down the problem you are trying to solve, then choose the resource that addresses it. Be cautious with highly technical advice that may not apply to your systems.

Curated resources

01

Nigeria-first guidance

01 / 01

Nigerian Communications Commission — Cyber Security Awareness

Best for: Business owners and staff who need straightforward reminders about online accounts, banking communications and personal information.

What you will find: Cybersecurity-awareness tips and reference materials published by the NCC.

How to use it: Pick one applicable topic for a short staff discussion. Some advice is broadly worded, so assess it in the context of your actual devices and services.

Official link: https://www.ncc.gov.ng/industry/cybersecurity Opens external site

01 / 02

Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team (ngCERT) — Alerts and Advisories

Best for: An owner, manager or technical helper who wants to understand newly reported threats.

What you will find: Nigerian cybersecurity advisories covering issues such as phishing, business-email impersonation, malware and software vulnerabilities.

How to use it: Check whether an advisory relates to an application, device or working practice your business actually uses. Many advisories target larger or more technical organisations; they are not instructions for every SME to follow.

Official link: https://cert.gov.ng/ Opens external site

01 / 03

Nigeria Data Protection Commission — Privacy Guidance

Best for: Businesses collecting customer phone numbers, addresses, identification records, staff information or other personal data.

What you will find: Information about Nigeria's data protection framework, privacy rights and organisational responsibilities.

How to use it: Read alongside CyberSafe's Data Protection guide. Legal requirements depend on your activities and circumstances; the regulator's resources are not a substitute for advice about your specific obligations.

Official link: https://ndpc.gov.ng/ Opens external site

02

Plain-language guides for everyday business security

02 / 01

UK National Cyber Security Centre — Small Organisations Guide

Best for: Owners of small businesses with little or no dedicated IT support.

What you will find: Short guidance on email, online accounts, devices, backups and recognising attacks.

How to use it: Choose a section that matches something you depend on each day. The cybersecurity principles are useful, but UK-specific reporting, laws and support schemes may not apply in Nigeria.

Official link: https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/small-organisations-guide-to-cyber-security Opens external site

02 / 02

NIST — Small Business Cybersecurity Corner

Best for: Business owners who want to go beyond individual tips and organise their security responsibilities.

What you will find: Guidance by topic, including phishing, employee awareness, multifactor authentication, data and device protection, and incident response.

How to use it: Start with one topic rather than attempting to read the whole library. NIST is a US standards institution; US reporting channels, government programmes and some regulatory references do not automatically apply in Nigeria.

Official link: https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber Opens external site

02 / 03

NIST — Small Business Cybersecurity: Non-Employer Firms

Best for: Sole proprietors and very small businesses that have limited resources and no dedicated security team.

What you will find: A November 2026 guide applying basic risk-management ideas to the smallest businesses, with suggestions that can be adapted as the business grows.

How to use it: Use it as a planning reference, not a certificate, regulatory compliance assessment or guarantee against attacks.

Official link: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/cswp/50/small-business-cybersecurity-non-employer-firms/final Opens external site

03

Help staff recognise suspicious requests

03 / 01

WhatsApp Help Center — Suspicious Messages and Scams

Best for: Businesses that receive orders, customer enquiries or payment-related messages through WhatsApp.

What you will find: Examples of suspicious message patterns and advice for handling unwanted or deceptive contacts.

How to use it: Discuss the examples with staff, then apply your business's independent verification process. A message without spelling mistakes can still be fraudulent.

Official link: https://faq.whatsapp.com/2286952358121083/ Opens external site

03 / 02

Microsoft Security — Cybersecurity Awareness Resources

Best for: Owners and staff seeking short explanations, downloadable awareness material and everyday protection tips.

What you will find: Guides about phishing, account protection, device security and online scams.

How to use it: Choose one short resource for a team briefing. Some links lead to Microsoft products or broader learning paths; using them is optional, and no purchase is required to read this directory.

Official link: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/business/cybersecurity-awareness Opens external site

03 / 03

UK NCSC — Suspected Phishing Email Exercise

Best for: A manager who wants staff to practise discussing suspicious messages.

What you will find: A short group activity about identifying and reporting possible phishing, designed to take around 15–30 minutes.

How to use it: Bring together a few staff members, work through the prompts and agree on one verification or reporting practice. UK-specific reporting references should be replaced with the appropriate Nigerian channels.

Official link: https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/section/exercise-in-a-box/suspected-phishing-email Opens external site

04

How to choose a trustworthy external resource

Check the web address. Open the official organisation or service website rather than relying on a forwarded document or shortened link. If a page asks you to download software, verify the source separately.

Check the intended audience. A technical vulnerability bulletin may be valuable to an IT professional but confusing to a shop owner who simply needs to secure a phone or account.

Check the date and product version. Advice about app settings, account recovery or supported operating systems can change. Use the provider's current instructions for the product you actually have.

Check the country. International advice about recognising phishing, using multifactor authentication and keeping backups can be useful in Nigeria. Foreign incident-reporting portals, consumer-protection procedures, laws, subsidies and certification schemes should not be assumed to apply here.

Avoid unnecessary disclosure. You should not need to upload customer lists, account passwords, bank statements or confidential business documents just to read public cybersecurity guidance.

05

Turn one resource into a useful business habit

You can hold a short discussion with the people who use your business accounts or handle money.

  1. Choose one problem: For example, unexpected WhatsApp verification requests or changes to supplier payment details.
  2. Read one relevant guide: Spend a few minutes reviewing its practical advice.
  3. Ask what happens in your business: Who receives the message? Who can authorise action? How would staff verify it?
  4. Agree on one action: Such as never sharing verification codes or confirming payment changes through an existing supplier contact.
  5. Review the rule later: Check that employees still know what to do, especially when someone new joins the business.

You do not need to turn this into an examination or ask staff to demonstrate harmful links. The point is to make decisions clearer before a real problem occurs.
