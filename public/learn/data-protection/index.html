Data Protection

Learn how to collect, use, store and share customer information responsibly, and understand what Nigeria's data protection law means for your business.

Explore the Customer Data Journey Understand the NDPA

That customer information you saved for delivery. Who else can see it?

A customer orders a product through WhatsApp and sends their name, phone number and delivery address.

You save the details, arrange delivery and complete the sale.

But what happens to that information afterwards?

Perhaps the address remains in a WhatsApp conversation. A copy is stored on an employee's phone, another appears in a spreadsheet, and the delivery rider still has the customer's contact details.

None of this necessarily means something has gone wrong. But it raises an important question: is your business keeping and sharing more customer information than it needs?

Protecting customer information is part of running a responsible business. You don't need a large IT department to begin, but you do need to know what information you hold, why you hold it and who can access it.

Rule to remember

COLLECT ONLY WHAT YOU NEED, USE IT FOR A CLEAR PURPOSE AND KEEP IT OUT OF UNNECESSARY HANDS.

What is personal data?

Personal data is information that identifies someone or could be used to identify them, directly or indirectly.

For a Nigerian small business, examples include:

  • A customer's name, phone number or delivery address.
  • Email addresses and customer contact records.
  • Photographs that identify people.
  • Employee contact details and payroll information.
  • Identification numbers or documents.
  • Order histories linked to identifiable customers.

Some personal data is particularly sensitive, including certain health information, biometric information and other categories protected under the law. This information may require additional safeguards and legal conditions.

Not every business record is personal data. A list of products or an anonymous total of daily sales may not identify anyone.

However, a sales record containing a customer's name, contact information and purchase details is personal data.

Data protection means handling such information lawfully, fairly, transparently and securely throughout the time your business needs it.

Why should your business care?

Customers trust you with their information

When someone shares their contact details or delivery address, they expect the information to be used responsibly.

A small mistake can expose several people

Sending a customer list to the wrong recipient or sharing it in an unnecessary WhatsApp group may expose information beyond its intended purpose.

Employees may have access they no longer need

Customer records can remain on personal phones, old spreadsheets or accounts previously used by former employees.

Poor records make problems harder to investigate

If you do not know where information is stored, you may struggle to respond when a customer requests a correction or reports a privacy concern.

Data protection is also a legal responsibility

Nigeria has a law governing how organisations collect, use and protect personal information. Businesses should understand the requirements relevant to their activities.

What does Nigeria's data protection law require?

The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA) establishes the principal legal framework for protecting personal data in Nigeria.

The law is overseen by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC). The Commission has also issued implementation guidance, including the General Application and Implementation Directive 2025.

The law covers personal data processing by organisations operating in Nigeria and certain other activities involving individuals in Nigeria.

Processing includes collecting, recording, storing, using, sharing, correcting and deleting personal information.

At a practical level, businesses should:

  • Have an appropriate lawful basis for handling personal data.
  • Explain why information is collected and how it will be used.
  • Collect only information necessary for the stated purpose.
  • Protect records against inappropriate access, loss or disclosure.
  • Keep information accurate where necessary.
  • Avoid retaining personal data longer than justified.
  • Respect applicable rights of the people whose information they hold.

Does every customer have to sign a consent form?

No. Consent is one lawful basis, but it is not the only one.

For example, processing necessary to fulfil a customer's order may be justified by the contractual relationship. Certain records may need to be kept to comply with legal obligations.

Other lawful bases may apply depending on the circumstances.

However, collecting a customer's phone number to deliver an order does not automatically give your business permission to add that person to unrelated promotional campaigns.

The appropriate lawful basis for marketing and other additional uses should be assessed separately.

Does every small business need NDPC registration?

Not necessarily. Registration and additional compliance requirements depend on how the business and its processing activities are classified under the applicable NDPC framework.

Do not assume that being a small business automatically removes your data protection responsibilities.

Businesses handling significant volumes of personal data, particularly sensitive information or higher-risk activities, should review their obligations carefully and seek appropriate professional guidance where needed.

Collect only the information your business actually needs

Before asking a customer for information, consider what is necessary to complete the task.

For example, a clothing business arranging delivery may need:

  • The customer's name or an appropriate delivery identifier.
  • A contact phone number.
  • The delivery location.
  • Details of the items being delivered.

It may not need the customer's date of birth, National Identification Number or photograph of an identity document.

Collecting extra information without a clear reason creates unnecessary work and additional privacy risk.

The same principle applies to employees. Information collected for recruitment, payroll or workplace administration should be relevant to its intended purpose.

Avoid asking customers for banking PINs, one-time passwords or account login details. These are not ordinary requirements for processing a sale.

Tell customers what you are doing with their information

Customers should be able to understand why your business collects their information.

A clear privacy notice can explain:

  • Who is collecting the information and how to contact the business.
  • Why the information is needed and the lawful basis for using it.
  • Who may receive it, such as an authorised delivery provider.
  • How long it will be retained or how that period is determined.
  • What rights customers have and how to make a request.
  • How they can raise a privacy concern.

For example, a short explanation during an order might say:

"We need your name, phone number and delivery address to prepare and deliver your order. We'll share the necessary delivery details with our delivery provider and handle your information according to our privacy notice."

This is an illustrative explanation, not a complete legal privacy notice.

A business website or online shop should provide an accessible privacy policy appropriate to its actual processing activities.

Store customer information somewhere you can control

Customer records may be kept in notebooks, spreadsheets, phones, email accounts or online business applications.

The format matters less than whether the information is protected appropriately.

Limit access

Only employees who need particular records should be able to view or change them.

A delivery employee may need details for assigned deliveries but not the business's complete customer database.

Protect the accounts and devices

Use strong authentication, device screen locks, software updates and appropriate access permissions.

Where supported, use separate employee accounts instead of one shared password.

Keep reliable backups

Important records should be backed up securely so the business can recover from accidental deletion, device failure or other disruption.

Backup copies also contain data and must be protected.

Avoid unnecessary copies

A customer list copied to several personal phones or chat groups can be difficult to control.

Choose an approved storage location and minimise copies outside it.

Protect paper records

Keep customer forms, staff files and other documents in a secure location. Dispose of them appropriately when no longer required.

Share information carefully

Businesses sometimes need to share information to provide a service.

A delivery rider may need a customer's name, phone number and delivery location.

An accountant may need appropriate financial records. A service provider may need access to information to operate a business system.

Before sharing, ask:

Who needs it?

Identify the person or service provider who requires the information.

What do they need?

Share only the details necessary for the task.

Why do they need it?

Make sure the disclosure is consistent with the stated purpose and an appropriate lawful basis.

How will it be protected?

Use an appropriate, authorised communication or access method and ensure recipients understand their responsibilities.

For example, a delivery rider assigned one order normally does not need the telephone numbers and addresses of every customer who ordered that week.

Where third-party companies process personal data on your behalf, additional contractual and legal requirements may apply.

If you use a cloud service that stores or transfers personal data outside Nigeria, the NDPA's cross-border transfer rules may also be relevant. Seek appropriate advice when necessary.

Know when information should be removed

Keeping every customer record forever is not a sensible data protection strategy.

Information should generally be retained only as long as there is an appropriate, lawful reason to keep it.

For example:

  • Delivery details may no longer be needed for day-to-day fulfilment after an order is completed.
  • Some transaction records may need to be retained for accounting, tax, contractual or legal reasons.
  • Employee information may have different retention requirements depending on its purpose.

There is no single retention period that applies to every type of information held by every business.

Set appropriate retention periods for different records, taking account of legal requirements and business needs.

When information is no longer needed, delete it securely or dispose of the physical records appropriately.

Remember that old copies may still exist in shared folders, backup systems or employees' devices. Retention and deletion procedures should account for these locations where appropriate.

What rights do customers have over their information?

The NDPA provides individuals with rights concerning their personal data, subject to applicable legal conditions and limitations.

These include rights relating to:

  • Being informed about how their information is used.
  • Accessing personal data held about them.
  • Correcting inaccurate information.
  • Objecting to certain processing activities.
  • Requesting deletion or restriction in applicable circumstances.
  • Withdrawing consent where consent is the lawful basis.

For example, a customer might ask your business to correct an outdated delivery address or stop using their number for promotional messages.

Your business should have a way to receive such requests, verify the identity and authority of the requester where necessary, and respond appropriately.

A deletion request does not always mean that every related record must be erased immediately. Some information may need to be retained for a valid legal reason.

For complex requests, seek guidance rather than ignoring them or disclosing information without proper verification.

What should you do if customer information is exposed?

Suppose a staff member sends a customer spreadsheet to the wrong person, a business phone containing customer records disappears or someone obtains unauthorised access to an online database.

The first step is to investigate what happened.

Step 1: Stop further exposure

Where possible, revoke inappropriate access, remove an unintended shared link or secure the affected account.

If a device is lost, use its supported remote security features where appropriate.

Do not assume that deleting a message guarantees that the recipient never saw or copied it.

Step 2: Inform the responsible person

Notify the business owner, manager or designated security contact promptly.

If an external service provider holds the data, contact them to establish what happened and what measures are available.

Step 3: Establish what information may be affected

Record the type of information involved, how many people may be affected, when the incident happened and whether the information was actually accessible to an unauthorised person.

Consider whether the exposure could lead to identity theft, fraud, unwanted contact or another form of harm.

Step 4: Preserve relevant evidence

Keep an incident record, messages, relevant access logs and details of the steps taken.

Do not unnecessarily circulate exposed personal information while investigating.

Step 5: Assess notification obligations promptly

Under Section 40 of the NDPA, a data controller must notify the NDPC within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach that is likely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms.

Where a breach is likely to result in a high risk to affected individuals, the Act also requires immediate communication to those individuals, subject to the applicable conditions.

These requirements depend on the circumstances. Not every misplaced document or missing device automatically meets the notification threshold.

If personal data may have been breached, assess the position promptly with qualified support where necessary. Do not delay containment while deciding whether reporting is required.

Step 6: Correct the underlying problem

Review why the incident occurred and what would reduce the chance of it happening again.

This might involve changing access permissions, improving staff instructions, restricting shared files or updating the business's data-handling procedure.

For official data protection information and privacy-related reporting, visit the Nigeria Data Protection Commission:

https://ndpc.gov.ng/

If the incident involves suspected criminal activity, financial fraud or unauthorised system access, other reporting channels and service providers may also need to be involved.

The customer list that went to the wrong people

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

It was a busy Wednesday morning at a small catering business in Abuja. Fatima was preparing delivery instructions for several lunch orders while the kitchen staff packed the food.

She had a spreadsheet containing customers' names, telephone numbers and delivery addresses. Rather than sending each rider the details of their assigned delivery, she accidentally shared the entire spreadsheet in the business's WhatsApp group.

A moment later, she realised that two former delivery assistants were still members of the group.

Her first instinct was to delete the message and hope nobody had opened it. She tried removing the file from the conversation, but couldn't be certain whether anyone had already downloaded a copy.

Fatima hesitated before telling the owner. She had only been trying to get the deliveries organised, and she was worried about being blamed.

When she finally explained what happened, the owner was concerned. Neither of them knew who might have saved the spreadsheet.

They contacted the group members, asked them not to retain or share the file, and recorded what information had been exposed. The owner also began checking whether the incident required further notification or professional advice.

There was no evidence that anyone had misused the information. But deleting the message had not necessarily removed every copy.

From then on, delivery assistants received only the customer details needed for their assigned orders.

What this story teaches: Customer information can spread beyond its intended recipients through ordinary workplace mistakes. Limiting what each person receives makes those mistakes less damaging.
