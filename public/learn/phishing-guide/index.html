Phishing: How Nigerian SMEs Are Targeted and What to Do About It

That urgent message from your boss. The supplier asking for a new account number. The login page that looks exactly like Microsoft 365.

Phishing is not a technical attack. It is a persuasion attack delivered by a message, and it remains the most common starting point for losses at small businesses in Nigeria — ahead of malware, ahead of anything involving code.

It succeeds for a simple reason: the messages are ordinary. They do not look like crime. They look like work.

The four you are most likely to receive

The changed account number

An email arrives inside a genuine invoice conversation. The supplier apologises — their old account is frozen, please pay into this new one. The thread is real, the tone is right, the amount matches. Only the account number is different.

This is the most expensive version, and the defence is procedural rather than technical: any change of payment details is confirmed by voice, on a number you already had, before anything is paid.

The manager in a hurry

A short message, apparently from the owner or MD, sent from an unfamiliar address or number. In a meeting. Cannot take calls. Needs a transfer or airtime now, will explain later. It targets the reluctance of a junior member of staff to question a senior one.

The counter is a rule the whole team knows: no payment happens on a message alone, no matter who it appears to come from, and nobody is ever in trouble for checking.

The login page that is almost right

A shared-document notification, a mailbox-full warning, a fake courier tracking page. The page it opens looks exactly like Microsoft 365 or Gmail — because it is a copy. Whatever you type goes straight to the attacker.

Check the address bar before typing. Better still, do not reach login pages through links at all: type the address yourself or use a bookmark. A password manager helps here too, because it will not autofill on a domain that does not match.

The OTP request

A call or message from "your bank", "WhatsApp support" or a customer with a payment problem, asking you to read out the code you just received. That code is the account.

Reading a message with cold eyes

  • Was it expected? Unexpected plus urgent is the combination that costs money.
  • Who actually sent it? Read the address, not the display name. Watch for a missing or extra character in the domain.
  • Where does the link go? Hover on a computer, press and hold on a phone.
  • What is it asking for? Credentials, codes and payment changes are the three requests that deserve a phone call.
  • Does the pressure make sense? Real deadlines rarely arrive with a threat attached.

The controls that reduce the damage

  1. Two-factor authentication on business email, then banking, then everything else. A stolen password stops being enough.
  2. A written payment-verification rule, agreed and repeated, covering changed account details and any transfer above a threshold you choose.
  3. Unique passwords, so one captured login does not open five doors.
  4. A reporting habit — same day, no blame. Campaigns hit several colleagues, so the first report protects the rest.
  5. Ten minutes a quarter walking the team through a real message you received.

If someone has already clicked

  1. Change that password immediately, from a different device, then sign out of all sessions.
  2. Check the mailbox for forwarding rules or filters the attacker may have added.
  3. If money moved, call the bank at once — speed is the only thing that makes a trace or reversal possible.
  4. Preserve evidence: the message, headers, screenshots, transaction references.
  5. Report to ngCERT (opens in a new tab), and to the EFCC (opens in a new tab) where funds are involved.
  6. Tell the team what the message looked like, the same day.
Verification is a two-minute phone call. Recovery, when it is possible at all, takes weeks.
