Cybersecurity Basics

You do not need to understand everything. You need to understand the few things that cause most of the damage.

Almost every incident that reaches a Nigerian small business comes down to one of four things: a password someone else now knows, a message someone believed, a payment nobody verified, or a device nobody updated. Get those four under control and you have dealt with the majority of realistic risk.

The four things

1. Accounts

Give every work account its own password, and turn on two-factor authentication starting with email. Email is the account that can reset all the others, which is why attackers go there first.

2. Messages

Treat any unexpected request that involves money, passwords or codes as unverified until you have confirmed it through a channel you chose. Not the number in the message, but the number you already had.

3. Payments

Confirm a credit in your own banking app before goods leave. Confirm any change of account details by phone. These two habits prevent the two most expensive frauds against SMEs here.

4. Devices and data

Install updates, use licensed software, lock screens, and keep a backup that is not permanently attached to the machine it protects.

Words you will keep meeting

  • Two-factor authentication (2FA): a second proof of identity after your password, usually a code from an app.
  • OTP: a one-time code sent to you. Never share it, with anyone, ever.
  • Phishing: a fake message designed to make you hand over access or money.
  • Malware: software running on your device for someone else's benefit.
  • Ransomware: malware that locks your files and demands payment.
  • Social engineering: manipulating a person instead of attacking a system.
  • Backup: a separate copy of your data you can restore from.

What to do this week

  1. Turn on 2FA for your business email.
  2. Turn on two-step verification for WhatsApp.
  3. Agree one payment-verification rule with your staff and say it out loud.
  4. Copy your most important folder to an external drive.

That is four tasks and perhaps an hour. Then work through the security checklist to see what is left.

Questions owners ask us most

What is phishing and how do I recognise it?

Phishing is a message (email, SMS or WhatsApp) built to look like it came from a trusted source such as your bank, a supplier or a delivery company. The signs are urgency you did not expect, a request for a password or OTP, a link whose address does not match the text, and a sender address that is nearly but not exactly right. When in doubt, contact the organisation on a number you already have.

Can ransomware really affect a business as small as mine?

Yes. Most ransomware is not aimed at anyone in particular. It spreads to whatever it can reach. It usually arrives through an attachment, an infected USB drive or software that has not been updated. Offline backups, current software and staff who recognise a suspicious file are what keep it survivable.

How do I create a strong password I can actually remember?

Use a passphrase: three or four unrelated words with a number and a symbol, such as Coffee#Lagos7Bridge. Length matters more than complexity. Better still, let a password manager generate and store them, so the only one you memorise is the master password.

My business WhatsApp was taken over. What do I do right now?

Re-register the number on your own phone to log the attacker out, then open Settings → Linked devices and end every session. Re-enable two-step verification, and tell customers on another channel to ignore any payment request sent while you were locked out. Report the incident to ngCERT at cert.gov.ng.

How do I tell whether a bank alert is real?

Do not judge the notification. Check the balance. Open your own banking app, not a link, and confirm the credit, the exact amount and the time. Alert screenshots and SMS sender names are both easy to fake.

Do I need antivirus if I only use my phone for business?

Phones are safer by default but not immune. Keep the operating system updated, install apps only from the official store, review app permissions occasionally, and set a screen lock and a SIM PIN. On Android, a reputable security app from the Play Store adds a useful layer.

What is the 3-2-1 backup rule?

Three copies of your important data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy kept offsite or offline. For a small business that can be as simple as your working files on the laptop, a weekly copy on an external drive, and an automatic sync to cloud storage.

How do I report a cybercrime in Nigeria?

ngCERT at cert.gov.ng handles cyber incidents and advisories. The EFCC at efcc.gov.ng handles online fraud and financial cybercrime. The Nigeria Police Force cybercrime unit can be reached through your nearest station. Preserve your evidence (screenshots, message threads and transaction records) before you report.
