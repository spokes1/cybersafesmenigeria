Employee Security

Help employees recognise suspicious requests, protect business information and know what to do when something goes wrong.

Staff Security Journey How to report concerns

Your employee receives an unusual request. Do they know who to ask?

An employee notices an unfamiliar sign-in alert while checking the business email. They want to report it, but nobody has explained who to contact.

At another desk, someone claiming to be your supplier sends a WhatsApp message asking for payment to a different bank account.

Both employees have a decision to make.

If they have never been told what to report, how to verify an unusual request or when to ask for help, they may make a mistake while trying to do their jobs.

Employee security begins with clear instructions. Your team should understand the risks connected to their work and feel comfortable asking questions before taking an unfamiliar action.

You do not need a large training budget or a dedicated IT department to establish these habits.

Rule to remember

GIVE STAFF CLEAR RULES, A TRUSTED PERSON TO ASK AND THE CONFIDENCE TO REPORT PROBLEMS EARLY.

What is employee security?

Employee security is the practice of helping everyone in a business use its accounts, devices, information and payment systems responsibly.

It includes teaching employees how to recognise suspicious requests, following agreed procedures, protecting customer information and reporting unusual activity.

It also involves giving people appropriate access to business systems and removing that access when they no longer need it.

The goal is not to turn every employee into a cybersecurity expert.

It is to make safe decisions part of normal business operations.

Why should your business care?

Employees handle important business transactions

Cashiers confirm customer payments. Office staff process invoices. Customer-service workers handle personal information and order details.

A mistaken decision can affect money, information or customer relationships.

Fraudulent requests may appear familiar

A criminal may impersonate a supplier, customer, manager or service provider. Staff need to know how to verify unusual requests without relying only on appearances.

Unclear responsibilities can lead to avoidable mistakes

If nobody knows who approves payments or handles suspicious messages, employees may make decisions beyond their responsibilities.

Early reporting can reduce further harm

An employee who quickly reports a suspicious link, lost device or unexpected account activity gives the business an opportunity to investigate and respond.

Staff changes can leave accounts exposed

When someone changes roles or leaves the business, their old permissions may no longer be appropriate.

Start security awareness on an employee's first day

Every new employee should receive a short explanation of the security practices relevant to their job.

A formal presentation is not necessary. A brief discussion and practical demonstration may be enough for a small business.

Cover these essentials.

Explain which accounts and devices they may use

Provide the accounts and permissions required for their responsibilities.

Where supported, each employee should use their own login rather than sharing a password.

Explain whether they may use personal phones or computers for business tasks, and what information must remain on approved business systems.

Explain the payment rules

A cashier should know how to verify an incoming payment before releasing goods.

An employee who pays suppliers should know that unexpected account changes require independent confirmation.

Make clear who is authorised to approve unusual or high-value payments.

Explain how to handle confidential information

Show employees where customer records, invoices and other business documents should be stored.

Tell them which information may be shared, with whom and through which approved channels.

Explain when to ask for help

Identify the owner, manager or designated person who handles suspicious messages, account problems and security incidents.

Make sure employees have a reliable way to contact that person.

Let employees practise a realistic situation

For example, ask:

"A customer shows you a successful transfer receipt, but the payment is missing from our bank account. What do you do?"

The employee should know that the sale remains pending while payment is independently verified.

Five habits every employee should practise

Verify unusual requests

Do not automatically trust a message because it appears to come from the business owner, a familiar supplier or an established customer.

If a request involves money, account access or confidential information, verify it through an established contact method.

An unexpected request for a verification code should never be accepted simply because the caller sounds official.

Protect passwords and accounts

Use the assigned account and approved authentication method.

Do not share passwords, PINs, one-time passwords or account recovery codes.

Lock computers and phones when leaving them unattended.

If a password or login code has been disclosed accidentally, report it promptly so the account can be secured.

Handle customer information carefully

Access only the customer information needed for your work.

Avoid copying customer records into personal email accounts, unnecessary WhatsApp groups or unapproved applications.

Check the intended recipient before sending invoices, customer lists or documents containing personal information.

Report accidental disclosures rather than attempting to conceal them.

Keep business devices safe

Install applications only through approved sources.

Avoid connecting unfamiliar USB drives or installing software sent by unexpected contacts.

Keep devices updated and notify the responsible person if a business phone or laptop is lost.

Employees using personal devices for authorised work should follow the business's agreed rules for device security and data access.

Report problems early

If something unusual happens, inform the appropriate person.

This includes suspicious messages, unexpected account changes, missing devices, unusual payment instructions and information sent to the wrong recipient.

Reporting a mistake early is more useful than waiting to see whether it causes a problem.

Different jobs need different security instructions

Not every employee faces the same risks.

A business can make security awareness more relevant by connecting it to what each person actually does.

Cashiers and sales attendants

Their main concerns include verifying transfers, recognising fake payment notifications and handling customer pressure.

They should know how to check payment records and when to hold a transaction for confirmation.

Customer-service and social media staff

These employees may manage WhatsApp Business conversations, Facebook Pages, Instagram enquiries or customer orders.

They should understand account security, customer privacy and how to respond to suspicious requests or imitation business profiles.

Office and administrative staff

They may receive invoices, maintain records and communicate with suppliers.

They should know how to verify changes to payment instructions, handle unexpected attachments and protect confidential documents.

Delivery and field employees

These workers may carry smartphones containing customer addresses, telephone numbers and order information.

They should know how to protect their devices and report loss, theft or accidental disclosure.

Managers and business owners

Their responsibilities include setting clear approval rules, managing permissions, providing reporting channels and ensuring security instructions are followed.

Senior employees should follow the same verification rules they expect others to follow.

Make reporting a simple process

An employee who notices something suspicious should not need to complete a complicated form before getting help.

Give employees an easy reporting process.

Step 1: Stop the activity

If a request appears suspicious, pause before sending money, entering a password or sharing information.

If something has already happened, stop further interaction with the suspicious message or service.

Step 2: Inform the responsible person

Contact the business owner, manager or designated IT support person using an established channel.

Explain what happened without guessing or hiding important details.

Step 3: Keep relevant information

Preserve the suspicious message, transaction reference, email or other relevant evidence where practical.

Avoid forwarding harmful links or attachments to colleagues unnecessarily.

Step 4: Follow the appropriate response

The next action depends on the situation.

A suspected account compromise may require securing passwords, sessions and recovery settings.

A lost business device may require contacting the mobile provider and protecting connected accounts.

A fraudulent bank transfer requires immediate contact with the bank or payment provider.

For suspected cybercrime in Nigeria, the business may report the incident to the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre through its official portal:

https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

Employees should know who handles external reporting on behalf of the business.

What if an employee has already made a mistake?

Suppose someone opens a suspicious attachment or enters a password into an unfamiliar website.

The employee may worry about being blamed and decide not to tell anyone.

That delay could make investigation and containment more difficult.

Business owners should encourage prompt, truthful reporting and investigate what happened fairly.

This does not mean employees have no responsibilities or that deliberate misconduct should be ignored.

It means an accidental mistake should be treated as an opportunity to contain the problem, understand why it occurred and improve the relevant procedure.

For example, if an employee followed fraudulent payment instructions, ask whether they had been trained to verify account changes and whether the approval procedure was clear.

The business may need a better process as well as further training.

Keep security training short and relevant

For a small business, occasional practical discussions may be more useful than lengthy presentations that employees struggle to remember.

Consider a short awareness discussion once a month or whenever an important new issue arises.

Choose one situation that employees might encounter, such as:

  • A false payment alert during a busy sales period.
  • A WhatsApp message asking for an account verification code.
  • A supplier requesting payment to an unfamiliar account.
  • A lost phone used for customer orders.

Explain what happened or could happen, ask how employees would respond and reinforce the correct procedure.

You can use examples from CyberSafe's Phishing Guide and other articles.

Avoid turning the session into a test designed to embarrass staff.

A useful discussion should leave everyone knowing what to check and whom to contact.

Make employee departures part of your security routine

Employees, contractors and freelancers may have access to important business accounts.

When their work ends, those permissions should be reviewed and removed at the appropriate time.

Identify the accounts and systems they used

Include email, cloud storage, social media, payment platforms, business applications and connected devices.

Preserve business information needed for handover

Arrange for important documents, customer conversations or work records to remain accessible to the business.

Follow applicable record-retention requirements and service-provider procedures.

Remove access

Disable individual accounts or revoke permissions at the appropriate time.

Review active sessions and connected applications where supported.

Secure shared access

If the departing employee knew a shared password, change it and review associated access.

Remove unnecessary WhatsApp linked devices and business-management permissions.

Recover business equipment

Collect business-owned phones, laptops, storage devices or other equipment where appropriate.

Update the account and equipment records after the handover.

These actions should be a routine part of staff departures rather than a response to suspected wrongdoing.

For detailed guidance, see our Account Security article.

The warning nobody knew who to report

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

Ruth had been working at a small event-decoration business in Benin City for just two weeks. Her responsibilities included responding to customer enquiries, preparing quotations and keeping track of bookings.

One Saturday morning, while checking the business email, she noticed a notification about a sign-in from an unfamiliar device.

She wasn't sure what to make of it. The owner sometimes checked emails from his phone, and Ruth had no way of knowing whether the notification was related to him.

There were several customer enquiries waiting, so she closed the notification and continued working.

Later that afternoon, she mentioned it while discussing a quotation with the owner.

“What sign-in notification?” he asked.

Ruth explained what she had seen. He told her he hadn't signed in from another device that morning.

They checked the account's recent activity using its security settings. They couldn't immediately establish whether someone had accessed the account without permission, but the uncertainty meant they had to spend time reviewing access and securing the account before returning to their usual work.

Ruth became uncomfortable.

“I wasn't sure it was important,” she said. “And I didn't want to disturb you over something that might be normal.”

The owner realised he had shown her how to prepare quotations and reply to customers, but had never explained what to do when something unusual happened.

There was no security contact, no reporting rule and no clear distinction between a routine computer problem and something that required immediate attention.

They agreed that unfamiliar account alerts and suspicious requests should be reported promptly, even when an employee wasn't certain there was a problem.

They also wrote down a simple reporting procedure for everyone working at the business.

They still couldn't say whether the original notification represented an attempted intrusion or a legitimate event they hadn't yet identified.

But the experience exposed a weakness in how the business prepared its staff.

What this story teaches: Employees shouldn't have to diagnose a cyberattack before reporting something unusual. Businesses need clear reporting instructions and an environment where people can raise concerns without fearing an unfair reaction.
