Email Security

Protect your business email, keep customer conversations private and recognise the warning signs when someone else may have access.

Secure your business email Review suspicious activity

What if someone else could read your business emails without you knowing?

Think about what passes through your business email every week. Customer enquiries, invoices, supplier conversations, receipts, staff information and sometimes links to important business accounts.

Now imagine someone gaining access to that mailbox.

They might read your conversations, collect information about your customers or send messages pretending to be you. They could even monitor an ongoing transaction and interfere when payment details are being discussed.

Your business email deserves the same attention you give your bank account. A few important security settings and good staff practices can make unauthorised access harder and help you recognise problems early.

Rule to remember

PROTECT YOUR EMAIL, CHECK WHO HAS ACCESS AND NEVER TRUST UNEXPECTED PAYMENT CHANGES WITHOUT VERIFICATION.

Why does business email need protection?

Email is often connected to several other parts of your business.

You might use it to sign in to cloud storage, manage social media accounts, communicate with suppliers or recover passwords when you forget them.

If someone gains unauthorised access, they may be able to:

  • Read confidential conversations with customers, employees and suppliers.
  • Send messages that appear to come from your business.
  • Attempt to reset passwords for other connected services.
  • Search for invoices, account information or customer records.
  • Create forwarding rules that send copies of incoming emails to another address.
  • Interfere with business transactions by introducing false instructions.

A compromised mailbox does not automatically give someone access to every other account. However, it may provide information or recovery options that make additional attacks possible.

How can someone gain access to your business email?

There are several common ways.

Reusing passwords

If you use the same password for different services, a password exposed elsewhere could also put your business email at risk.

Phishing messages

A fraudulent email or website may persuade you to enter your email password or approve an unfamiliar sign-in request.

Harmful software

Certain types of malicious software can attempt to steal passwords or authentication information from an infected device.

Shared login details

When several employees know the same email password, it becomes difficult to control who has access or determine who performed an action.

Access that was never removed

A former employee or outside contractor may retain access if their account or permissions were not properly removed.

Untrusted connected applications

An application given permission to access email may be able to read or manage messages. If that access is unnecessary or misused, business information may be exposed.

You may not be able to prevent every attempted attack, but you can reduce the opportunities for someone to gain or retain access.

Secure your business email in five steps

You can start with these five actions, whether your business uses Gmail, Outlook, Microsoft 365 or another reputable email provider.

1. Unique password

A password not reused elsewhere.

2. Multi-factor authentication

Another sign-in check, preferably phishing-resistant where supported.

3. Recovery access

Trusted recovery methods controlled by authorised people.

4. Account review

Devices, forwarding rules and connected apps.

5. Staff access

Individual permissions and prompt access removal.

Step 1: Give your email a strong, unique password

Your business email should have a password that is not used for banking, social media or other services.

Avoid predictable passwords containing your business name, phone number or common word combinations.

A reputable password manager can help you create and store strong, unique passwords without having to memorise every one.

If you suspect that your email password has already been exposed, change it using the official account settings.

Step 2: Enable multi-factor authentication

Multi-factor authentication, or MFA, adds an extra check when someone signs in.

For example, after entering a password, you may need to confirm the sign-in through an authenticator app or another approved method.

Where supported, a passkey or security key can provide stronger protection against many phishing attempts.

An authenticator app is generally preferable to relying only on SMS codes when stronger options are available.

Never approve a sign-in request you did not initiate, and never share a verification code with someone who contacts you unexpectedly.

Where to begin:

  • Gmail or Google Workspace: Open your Google Account security settings and look for 2-Step Verification.
  • Microsoft 365 or Outlook: Open the account's security information or sign-in settings and review the available authentication methods.

For organisation-managed accounts, your administrator may need to enable or manage these settings.

Step 3: Secure your account recovery options

Your recovery email address, phone number and backup sign-in methods help you regain access when you are locked out.

Check that the recovery information belongs to you or to an appropriately authorised business contact.

Remove unfamiliar recovery details.

If your provider offers recovery codes, keep them in a secure place that is not dependent on access to the email account itself.

For a business with several employees, establish who is authorised to manage account recovery.

A mailbox should not depend entirely on the personal phone number of an employee who may eventually leave the organisation.

Step 4: Review sign-ins, forwarding rules and connected applications

This is one of the most useful checks because a mailbox may still appear to function normally while information is being redirected.

Look for:

  • Sign-ins from devices or locations you do not recognise.
  • Automatic forwarding to unfamiliar email addresses.
  • Rules that move messages into unusual folders or delete them.
  • Applications connected to the mailbox that you no longer use or recognise.
  • Changes to recovery settings or authentication methods that you did not make.

For Gmail:

Open Gmail settings and review the forwarding and filter sections. You can also use your Google Account security settings to review devices, recent security activity and connected applications.

For Outlook or Microsoft 365:

Review Mail settings for rules and forwarding options. Check recent account sign-in activity through the official Microsoft account or work-account security portal.

For business-managed Microsoft 365 accounts, an administrator may need to investigate additional mailbox rules, permissions and connected applications.

Menu names may vary depending on your account type and the version of the service.

If you find a setting you cannot explain, investigate it rather than assuming it is harmless.

Step 5: Control who can access business email

Every employee who needs email access should ideally have an individual account or a separately assigned permission.

Avoid giving several employees the same password.

If people need to handle a shared address such as sales@yourbusiness.com, use an authorised shared-mailbox or delegation feature where your provider supports it.

This allows people to work with the mailbox using their own accounts rather than sharing one login.

Review access whenever someone changes responsibilities or leaves the business.

Remove unnecessary permissions promptly, including access held by former employees, contractors and outside service providers.

How do you know if someone may have accessed your email?

A compromised email account may not show an obvious warning.

Watch for changes such as:

Messages you did not send

Customers or colleagues report receiving strange requests from your email address, or you find unfamiliar messages in your Sent folder.

Emails disappearing or arriving in unusual folders

Important messages seem to vanish, particularly invoices, payment discussions or security notifications.

Unexpected forwarding or inbox rules

Your account contains instructions to forward, delete or move messages that you did not create.

Unfamiliar login activity

Your provider reports a sign-in from a device or location you do not recognise.

A different location alone is not proof of compromise, but it deserves investigation.

Changes to account recovery information

Your recovery phone number, email address or authentication method has changed without your permission.

Difficulty signing in

Your normal password suddenly stops working, or you receive password-reset notifications you did not request.

One sign does not always mean your mailbox has been compromised. However, unexpected account activity should be checked promptly.

Read and handle business emails more safely

Securing your account is important, but employees also need to know how to handle suspicious messages.

Before opening an unexpected attachment, sharing confidential information or following unusual instructions, check what the sender is asking you to do.

A display name can be copied. A familiar email address may also belong to an account that has been compromised.

Be particularly careful when a message asks you to:

  • Share passwords, verification codes or customer information.
  • Sign in through an unexpected link.
  • Download or enable content in an unfamiliar attachment.
  • Send money to a new bank account.
  • Keep an unusual payment request confidential.

When a request involves payments or sensitive business information, confirm it through a separate, trusted channel.

What if your business uses its own email domain?

Some businesses use addresses such as info@yourbusiness.com.ng instead of personal email accounts.

A business domain can make it easier to manage staff email accounts centrally and control who uses your business identity.

However, simply owning a domain does not automatically make your email secure.

If your business sends email using its own domain, ask your email provider or technical support team about three important protections:

SPF (Sender Policy Framework)

Helps receiving email systems identify which servers are authorised to send mail for your domain.

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)

Adds a digital signature that receiving systems can use to help verify that an email was authorised by the sending domain and has not been altered in transit.

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance)

Works with SPF and DKIM to help receiving systems identify and handle certain messages that misuse your domain.

Together, these controls can reduce some forms of email impersonation and improve how receiving services assess messages from your domain.

They do not prevent every phishing attack or stop someone from using a compromised legitimate mailbox.

Configuration normally involves your email provider and domain's DNS settings. If you are unfamiliar with these settings, ask a qualified administrator or hosting provider for help.

What should you do if your email account has been compromised?

If you believe someone has gained access to your business email, take action promptly.

1. Secure access to the account

Use a trusted device and open your email provider's official website or application.

Change the password and use the provider's account-recovery process if you can no longer sign in.

If the account is managed by your business, notify the administrator. They may need to temporarily block access while investigating.

2. End unauthorised sessions

Sign out unfamiliar devices and revoke active sessions where the service allows it.

Changing the password alone may not invalidate every existing session or connected application's access.

3. Check authentication and recovery settings

Remove unfamiliar recovery information and authentication methods.

Confirm that your authorised recovery options are still under your control.

4. Examine forwarding rules and connected applications

Look for unknown forwarding addresses, inbox rules, delegated access and third-party application permissions.

Remove or disable suspicious settings after recording relevant evidence. For organisation-managed accounts, ask an administrator to check for hidden rules or permissions that may not be visible in ordinary mailbox settings.

5. Review what may have been accessed or sent

Check recent sign-ins, sent messages, deleted items and relevant account logs where available.

Determine whether invoices, customer records or other sensitive information may have been exposed.

6. Warn relevant people where necessary

If suspicious messages were sent from your account, inform affected customers, suppliers or employees through a trusted alternative channel.

Ask them to disregard fraudulent instructions and independently verify payment requests.

If customer or employee information may have been exposed, seek appropriate guidance on any notification or legal obligations that apply.

7. Report financial or security incidents

If suspicious emails involved payment instructions, contact your bank or payment provider promptly and confirm that no unauthorised transactions have occurred.

You can also report cybersecurity incidents to the Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team through its official website:

https://cert.gov.ng/

For suspected cybercrime, the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre provides a reporting portal:

https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

Keep relevant messages, transaction records and incident details for investigation.

Recovery cannot be guaranteed, but prompt reporting and containment may limit further harm.

A few rules every employee should know

Business email security becomes easier when employees understand what is expected of them.

  • Use your own authorised login rather than sharing someone else's email password.
  • Report unexpected sign-in alerts, unusual forwarding settings or messages you did not send.
  • Verify changes to supplier payment details using a trusted contact method.
  • Do not send customer records or confidential business information to personal accounts without authorisation.
  • Inform the appropriate person immediately if you suspect someone else has accessed your mailbox.

If you're a small business owner working alone, these same rules still apply. Set aside time periodically to review your account security and ensure you can recover access when needed.

The customer reply that never reached the inbox

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

Kunle operated a small printing business in Lagos. Most customers sent their designs, quotations and payment enquiries through the business email address.

One afternoon, a regular customer called to complain that nobody had responded to an urgent printing request.

Kunle insisted that no message had arrived. The customer, equally frustrated, said they had replied to the quotation that morning.

When Kunle checked the email account again, he found the customer's message in an unexpected folder.

He initially assumed someone had moved it by mistake. But while looking through the mailbox settings, he noticed a rule that automatically moved messages containing certain invoice references away from the inbox.

He couldn't remember creating it.

Kunle wasn't particularly familiar with email security settings. He had originally configured the account himself and had rarely checked anything beyond the inbox.

He also noticed an unfamiliar sign-in entry, although he couldn't establish whether it belonged to him using another device.

Rather than ignore the uncertainty, he used the email provider's official security instructions to begin securing the account and sought help reviewing its settings.

He contacted the customer by telephone to confirm the outstanding job.

By then, the customer's deadline had become difficult to meet. Kunle also had to review other conversations to understand whether important messages had been missed.

He couldn't immediately establish who had created the rule or whether anyone had accessed private information.

But the experience showed him that an email account could appear to work normally while important messages were being handled in unexpected ways.

What this story teaches: A missing email may have an ordinary explanation, but unfamiliar mailbox rules or sign-in activity deserve investigation. Businesses should know how to check who has access to their email accounts.

  1. Customer says their reply was sent
  2. Kunle finds the message in an unexpected folder
  3. Unfamiliar rule and sign-in entry require investigation
  4. Account security checked and customer called directly

Your five-step business email security review

Use this as a short visual action guide, not an interactive checklist.

1. Protect the password
Give your business email its own strong password.

2. Add another sign-in check
Enable multi-factor authentication, using stronger methods where supported.

3. Confirm recovery access
Make sure your recovery options are correct and controlled by authorised people.

4. Look for hidden access
Review recent sign-ins, forwarding rules, mailbox permissions and connected applications.

5. Review staff permissions
Give employees appropriate access and remove it when no longer needed.
