Fake bank alerts

A credit alert on a screen is a claim, not a confirmation. Only your account balance is proof.

A customer shows a debit alert, a transfer receipt or an SMS that looks exactly like your bank's. The goods leave the shop. The money never arrives.

This is one of the most costly frauds against Nigerian retail and wholesale businesses, and it does not need any hacking at all. Alert screenshots are trivial to fake, and so are SMS sender names.

How it plays out

  • A screenshot of a successful transfer, sometimes edited from a real earlier transaction.
  • An SMS that appears to come from your bank's usual sender ID.
  • A "network delay" story: the money is on the way, the bank is slow today, the customer is in a hurry.
  • Pressure applied at closing time, during a rush, or when the owner is not on site.

The rule that stops it

Nothing leaves the premises until the credit appears in your own account, checked by you, in your own banking app or on your own alert channel.

  • Check the balance, not the notification. Open the app yourself and confirm the new balance.
  • Match the amount and the exact time. Partial or older transactions get reused.
  • Give one person the authority to confirm payments, and make it clear that no staff member is ever in trouble for making a customer wait.
  • Be extra careful with transfers just under your usual approval threshold.

If it already happened

  1. Preserve everything: the screenshot, the phone number, the CCTV time, the order record.
  2. Report to your bank immediately — some transfers can still be traced or reversed if flagged early.
  3. Report the fraud to the EFCC (opens in a new tab) and to the police, with your evidence attached.
  4. Brief every staff member on exactly how the request was framed, so the next attempt is recognised.
