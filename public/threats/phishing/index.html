Phishing

The most common way a small business gets compromised is still a message that someone believed.

Phishing is a message built to look like it came from somewhere you trust — your bank, a supplier, a courier, or your own manager. The goal is to get you to hand over a password or a one-time code, approve a payment, or open a file.

It works because it does not feel like an attack. It feels like a normal Tuesday morning request that you are slightly late replying to.

How it usually arrives

  • An email that copies a familiar login page. You type your password into a page that is not the real one.
  • A WhatsApp or SMS message with a shortened link and a deadline: "confirm before 5pm or your account is restricted".
  • An attachment named like an invoice, a waybill or a bank statement.
  • A call that follows the message, from someone who already knows your name and your company.

What gives it away

  • Urgency you did not expect. Real institutions rarely give you fifteen minutes.
  • A reply address that is close but not right — a swapped letter, an extra word, a different domain ending.
  • A request for an OTP, a PIN or a password. No bank and no legitimate service ever needs these from you.
  • A link whose destination does not match the text. On a phone, press and hold to preview it before tapping.
  • A tone that does not match the person. A manager who never says "kindly treat as urgent" suddenly does.

What to do

  1. Do not reply in the same thread. Contact the person on a number or address you already had.
  2. If a password was entered, change it immediately on the real site, then change it anywhere else it was reused.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication on email first. It is the account that resets all the others.
  4. Tell your team the same day. Phishing runs usually hit several people in one company.
  5. Report it to ngCERT (opens in a new tab) so the campaign can be tracked.
If a message pressures you to act before you can verify it, the pressure is the attack.
