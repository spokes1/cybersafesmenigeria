Ransomware.

Your files are locked. Can your business still operate?

Learn what ransomware can do to orders, customer records and business systems, and how to prepare for recovery without depending on a criminal's promise.

The morning your records stop opening

Imagine arriving at work to find that your order spreadsheet, invoices and customer files will no longer open. A message on the computer says your files have been locked and demands payment to restore access.

You still have customers waiting for deliveries. Staff are asking what to do. You cannot even be sure whether your latest backup is safe.

Ransomware turns a technology problem into a business problem very quickly. The first decisions matter: preventing further damage, protecting any usable backups and deciding what work can safely continue.

You don't need to know the name of the ransomware to take those first steps.

Rule to remember

STOP USING AFFECTED SYSTEMS, ISOLATE THEM WHERE SAFE, PROTECT YOUR BACKUPS AND GET TRUSTED HELP BEFORE RESTORING ANYTHING.

What is ransomware?

Ransomware is a form of malicious software, or an associated cyberattack, used to extort money from victims. It often encrypts files so their owners cannot open them. Criminals may demand payment in exchange for a decryption key or a promise not to publish stolen information.

Some attacks also involve stealing files before or instead of encrypting them. That means a business may have a data-exposure problem even if it succeeds in restoring its records.

Ransomware is related to malware, but this page focuses on the business disruption, extortion and recovery decisions that follow an attack.

Encryption is a way of making information unreadable without the appropriate key. Encryption used by legitimate services helps protect information; in ransomware, criminals use it to deny you access.

A ransom message may mention a deadline or claim that files will be deleted or published. Those claims should be treated seriously, but they are not independent proof of what has happened. The business must investigate.

What can ransomware interrupt in a small business?

Orders and delivery records. A wholesaler may lose access to the spreadsheet that says what customers ordered or what has already been dispatched.

Invoices and accounts. A small office may struggle to check outstanding payments or prepare accurate bills.

Customer or employee information. Personal records may be inaccessible, stolen or both. That can create privacy and reporting obligations.

Shared files and connected devices. If employees work from shared folders or connected storage, more than one device or account may be affected.

Daily income. Staff time may be diverted to reconstructing records, reassuring customers and arranging recovery, even when the business is not permanently shut down.

Not every ransomware incident affects all these areas. The impact depends on the systems involved, what the attacker accessed and how well the business prepared.

How can it reach a business?

  • Deceptive downloads and attachments. A person may be tricked into opening a harmful installer, document or link.
  • Stolen passwords and exposed remote access. Criminals may use compromised credentials or weaknesses in remotely accessible systems.
  • Unpatched applications. Attackers can exploit security weaknesses in software or devices that haven't been updated.
  • Unauthorised access through suppliers or service providers. A connected account or service can sometimes become a route into another business.

You may not know the entry point when the first ransom note appears. Determining it is part of the investigation, not something an employee should guess at.

What might you notice?

  • Documents that previously opened now show errors or have unfamiliar file extensions.
  • A note on the screen or in a folder demands money to recover files.
  • Several shared folders or devices become inaccessible around the same time.
  • Security tools flag suspicious encryption or unusual file activity.
  • A criminal claims to have copied customer information and threatens to publish it.

A missing file or broken computer does not automatically mean ransomware. Likewise, stolen data may not produce an obvious warning. Treat these as reasons to investigate promptly.

What to do if ransomware is suspected

1. Pause affected work and tell the responsible person

Stop opening files, running unfamiliar repair tools and using affected devices for customer information, banking or sensitive business activity. Alert the owner, manager or designated support contact immediately.

2. Isolate affected devices where safe and practical

Disconnect affected computers from Wi-Fi, mobile hotspots or network cables to reduce possible spread. Keep other employees from using shared resources that may be affected.

If a device manages an essential system or isolation is complicated, seek competent help so you do not create additional safety or operational problems. Avoid unnecessary shutdowns or wiping devices before evidence and recovery requirements are considered; a specialist may need to advise on power-off decisions.

3. Protect backups from the affected environment

Do not plug a backup drive into a computer you suspect is compromised. If a backup service or storage device might be reachable from the affected account or network, ask the person responsible for it to protect access without destroying useful evidence.

Do not assume that a connected USB drive or cloud-synchronised folder is clean simply because it is called a backup.

4. Record what happened

Keep the ransom note and relevant screenshots if it is safe to collect them. Note which devices or files were affected, when the problem was noticed, and who had access. Do not delete the note or repeatedly experiment with unknown decryption software.

5. Use trusted help and report the incident

If the business has no IT staff, seek a reputable professional through a previously trusted contact or a carefully verified recommendation. You may also contact ngCERT for guidance and report suspected cybercrime to the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre using its official reporting service.

6. Assess accounts, information and business obligations

Use a separate trusted device to review exposed account access and change credentials if needed. Investigate whether customer or employee information may have been taken, not only whether files were encrypted. Seek appropriate guidance on data-protection and notification requirements where personal information may be involved.

7. Restore only after the threat is addressed

Recovery may involve rebuilding affected devices, checking that backup copies are usable and clean, restoring records in a prioritised order, and monitoring for further suspicious activity. Avoid reconnecting a device to normal operations simply because the files start opening again.

Should you pay the ransom?

Paying is not recommended. The criminal may not provide a working key, may demand more money or may still release information they copied. Payment does not make an infected device safe and is not a substitute for incident response.

If your business is facing an extortion demand, seek qualified incident-response and legal advice about the situation and any applicable obligations. Do not rely on assurances from the attacker.

There are sometimes legitimate free decryption tools for particular ransomware families, including tools listed by the No More Ransom project. They do not cover every attack. A trusted specialist should confirm the ransomware family and safe recovery approach before you download or run a tool.

Can the business keep operating while systems are down?

Possibly, but only through information and equipment that are safe to use.

Customer enquiries: If your business has a separate, unaffected phone and approved contact records, you may be able to give customers accurate updates. Do not copy possibly compromised contact lists to an unprotected personal account just to keep working.

Orders and delivery: Paper order books, previously verified receipts or independently available records may help reconstruct essential jobs. Label uncertain information clearly and recheck details with customers where needed.

Payments: Use only independently verified banking channels on trusted devices. Never make payments from an affected computer merely to meet a deadline.

Backups and records: Keep backup media separate until someone has assessed it. Restoring or synchronising too early could bring the problem back or destroy evidence.

A temporary workaround is not proof that systems are clean. Decide what work can safely continue with the information you have.

Prepare now, before files stop opening

Decide which records matter most

Make a short list of information you could not easily recreate: customer orders, contact records, invoices, stock records, payroll details and essential business documents. Know where these are stored.

Back up those records regularly

Keep protected backup copies separate from devices and accounts that could be compromised. Where practical, use offline or otherwise isolated backup storage. A permanently attached drive or ordinary synchronised folder can also be affected.

Test that you can restore a file

A backup is useful only if the business can recover the information it needs. Periodically restore a sample file safely and check that it opens, is complete and is recent enough.

Update and restrict access

Keep operating systems and applications supported and updated. Use strong, unique sign-ins and multi-factor authentication where available, especially for remote access. Limit administrator privileges and remove permissions employees no longer need.

Put one response contact and one fallback plan in writing

Employees should know whom to tell when something looks wrong, which equipment should be left alone and where the business keeps independent copies of essential contact and recovery information.

Ask one practical question: If our main business computer could not be used tomorrow, what could we safely do and what would have to stop?

ILLUSTRATIVE STORY · ILORIN

Illustrative story: The orders waiting in Ilorin

Fictional business scenario. It is not a report of a real incident.

On a Friday morning in Ilorin, Halima opened the stock computer at the spare-parts shop where she worked. Several dealers were expecting deliveries before the weekend, but the order spreadsheet would not open. The filenames looked unfamiliar, and a message demanded payment to unlock them.

The owner, Ayo, was already on his way to make deliveries. When Halima called, his first question was whether the shop could still print the invoices. It couldn't. They needed the same computer to check which orders had been paid for.

Ayo told Halima to leave the computer alone until he got back. He hoped the problem was just a file error, but when he arrived and saw the payment demand, he understood that trying random fixes could make matters worse.

His next thought was the backup drive. It usually stayed in the desk drawer, but he couldn't remember when anyone had last copied the stock records to it. Halima suggested plugging it in to find out. They decided not to connect it to the affected computer.

Two customers were already asking whether their orders had left the shop. Ayo searched the paper delivery book, while Halima used an unaffected phone to check earlier customer conversations. Some quantities matched. Others did not, and they were unwilling to dispatch the wrong parts just to meet the deadline.

There was no IT department to call. Ayo contacted another shop owner for a recommendation and arranged for someone qualified to examine the machine. In the meantime, they kept the affected computer isolated and documented the unusual message.

By the end of the day, they had confirmed a few orders but postponed others. They still did not know whether customer information had been copied, whether the backup was usable or how long recovery would take.

The incident exposed how much depended on one computer and how little the business knew about its backups. Ayo decided that future orders would need a clearer fallback record and tested copies that weren't always connected to the shop's daily systems.

What this story teaches: Ransomware can interrupt a business even before anyone establishes the full extent of the attack. Protecting separate backups and knowing how to keep essential operations running safely matters as much as recognising the ransom demand.

Practical takeaway: Your business continuity board

If you lose access to your main computer, can you answer four questions?

What must stop? Identify affected systems and sensitive tasks that should not continue.

What information is still trustworthy? Look for separate, protected records that haven't been exposed to the affected environment.

What can continue safely? Consider customer updates and essential orders using known-safe channels and verified information.

What comes back first? Prioritise clean restoration of records and services that keep the business running.

Start here

Choose your most important business computer or account. Identify the records you would lose access to, find out where its backup is stored and test that one essential file can be restored safely.
