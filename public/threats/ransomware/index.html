Ransomware

Ransomware does not care how small your business is. It cares whether you can restore without paying.

Ransomware encrypts your files and leaves a demand for payment, usually in cryptocurrency. Records, invoices, customer data and photographs all become unreadable at once, and the business stops.

It typically arrives through a malicious attachment, an infected USB drive, or unpatched software exposed to the internet. Increasingly, attackers also copy data before locking it and threaten to publish it.

The honest position on paying

Paying funds the next attack and does not guarantee a working key. It is also not a recovery plan. The businesses that come through this well are the ones that can rebuild from a backup the attacker could not reach.

Preparation that works

  1. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of important data, on two kinds of storage, with one kept offsite or offline.
  2. Test a restore. An untested backup is a hope, not a backup. Restore one folder this month and confirm it opens.
  3. Keep at least one backup disconnected. Anything permanently mounted gets encrypted along with the original.
  4. Patch operating systems and business software promptly.
  5. Limit administrator accounts and remove access for staff who have left.

If it happens

  1. Disconnect affected machines from the network and from any external drives, but do not wipe them.
  2. Do not pay, and do not delete the ransom note. It helps identify the variant.
  3. Report to ngCERT (opens in a new tab); in some cases a free decryptor exists for the specific family.
  4. Rebuild from a clean backup, and change every password afterwards.
