Social Engineering

A convincing caller, a helpful visitor or a familiar WhatsApp contact can persuade someone to bypass a security rule. Learn to recognise the pressure and respond safely.

UNEXPECTED REQUESTA caller asks for access
BUSINESS PROCEDUREPause · Check · Decide

Being helpful does not mean giving someone access.

The caller sounds helpful. Why are they rushing you?

The phone rings at a small business. The caller says there is a problem with the shop's order system.

They know the shop's name. They sound professional. They offer to fix everything in five minutes.

Then they ask the employee to install a remote-support application and share an access code.

The employee wants to help the business. The caller wants the employee to act before checking who they are.

That difference is what makes social engineering dangerous. The attack often begins with a believable conversation, not an obviously suspicious website.

Rule to remember

Being helpful does not mean giving someone access. Pause, verify who is asking, and follow your business's normal procedure.

What is social engineering?

Social engineering is the deliberate use of deception or manipulation to persuade someone to do something that puts money, information, accounts, devices or physical premises at risk.

The person may claim to be a manager, bank representative, customer, supplier, delivery service, technician or fellow employee. They may sound friendly and informed rather than threatening.

The request might be to reveal a one-time code, open a door, share a customer list, approve a transaction, install software or change an account setting.

Social engineering can happen through telephone calls, WhatsApp, SMS, email, social media and face-to-face conversations. It can also be combined with a compromised account or a malicious website.

Phishing is one form of social engineering. Business Email Compromise is another related threat. This page looks more broadly at the manipulation behind those requests, especially when an employee is speaking to someone in real time.

The tactics behind the story

Authority: "I'm the person in charge."

The caller claims to be the owner, a bank official, an auditor or an engineer. A title or uniform may sound convincing, but it is not proof of authorisation.

Urgency and fear: "Do it before something goes wrong."

A deadline, threatened account suspension or possible delivery failure can make someone rush. An actual urgent problem still permits an independent check.

Familiarity: "I know your business."

The person refers to real employee names, recent posts, products or supplier relationships. Some of this information may be publicly available or obtained elsewhere.

Helpfulness and reciprocity: "Let me fix this for you."

An unexpected offer of support or a small favour can make the next request feel reasonable. A helpful tone is not evidence of trustworthiness.

Secrecy or isolation: "Don't involve anyone else."

A request to bypass a manager, ignore a normal approval step or keep the conversation private removes the opportunity for someone else to check.

These are warning signs, not automatic proof of fraud. Look closely at what the person wants you to do and whether they will allow the normal verification process.

Where can it happen in a small business?

An unexpected phone call. Someone impersonates a bank or technical-support representative and asks for passwords, one-time codes, device access or account changes. The displayed number can be falsified.

A WhatsApp message. A person appears to be a known colleague or service provider and requests customer data, money or an account code. The account may be fake or compromised.

An in-person visit. Someone claiming to be a technician, delivery agent or contractor asks to enter a staff-only room, inspect a business device or collect records without a confirmed appointment.

A message with a shortcut. Someone offers a refund, delivery update, quick repair or special opportunity, then directs the employee to an unexpected link, QR code or application.

A convincing recorded voice. Audio can be imitated or manipulated. A recognisable voice alone should not override a payment, account-access or information-sharing procedure.

These examples cover different channels, but they share one idea: a claimed identity is being used to obtain an action that the business should verify.

How to recognise the moment to stop

Ask whether the request would cross any of these boundaries:

  • Money: Are you being asked to send funds, refund a customer, or change payment instructions unexpectedly?
  • Account access: Are you being asked for a password, login code, recovery code, linked-device approval or new user permission?
  • Business information: Are you being asked for staff records, customer lists, orders or documents that the person does not normally receive?
  • Devices or premises: Are you being asked to install software, share remote access, change security settings or admit an unverified visitor into a restricted area?

If the answer is yes, stop and verify before acting. A convincing story does not replace authorisation.

A practical response: Pause, check, decide

1. Pause. Don't provide the code, information, payment or access while you are under pressure. You may say you need to follow the company's normal process.

2. Check independently. Use an existing trusted number, bookmarked official service portal or previously established contact record. Do not use only the contact details, link or callback number supplied by the person making the request.

3. Ask the right person. If the action affects money, customer information or an important account, involve the manager or authorised staff member. A real support provider should be able to work with your approved process.

4. Decide from verified facts. Proceed only if the identity, request and permission are confirmed. If you cannot confirm them, leave the request pending.

5. Report the concern. Inform the business's responsible person and preserve relevant messages or call information where practical. Avoid forwarding suspicious attachments or links unnecessarily.

Helpful sentences staff can use

  • "I need to verify this through our normal contact number."
  • "I can't give out codes or passwords on a call."
  • "Our manager must approve access to that system."
  • "I'll call the service provider back through our existing support channel."

These are not confrontational statements. They allow employees to remain polite while protecting the business.

If someone has already followed the instructions

The response depends on what was disclosed or changed. Act promptly and tell the responsible person rather than trying to hide the incident.

If a password or verification code was shared: Secure the affected account through its official service or administrator. Change compromised credentials, review active sessions and recovery settings, and check for unauthorised changes. Use a trusted device if the original device may be compromised.

If remote access was granted or software installed: Stop the session if possible, alert your IT support or service provider, and isolate the device if advised or if there are signs of compromise. Don't assume removing the app alone resolves the problem. Have the device and any exposed accounts assessed before returning them to normal use.

If money was sent: Contact the originating bank or payment provider immediately through official channels. Provide accurate transaction details and ask what urgent fraud-response or recall steps are available. Recovery is not guaranteed.

If customer or employee information was disclosed: Restrict further access where possible, preserve evidence, identify what information may be involved and assess any relevant data protection and notification obligations.

If someone gained physical access: Inform the owner or manager, secure the affected area, review what the visitor could reach and preserve relevant visitor records or CCTV footage where lawfully available.

Suspected cybercrime in Nigeria can be reported to the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre through its official e-reporting portal: https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/ .

The call that came before closing time

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

Ada was attending to customers at a fashion shop in Port Harcourt when someone called, claiming to be from the company that provided the shop's online ordering system.

The caller knew the shop's name and warned that a technical problem might cause some orders to disappear. He offered to fix it immediately if Ada installed a remote-support application and shared a connection code.

It was getting close to closing time, and several customers were still waiting for updates on their orders. Ada didn't want to be responsible for losing their information.

She considered following the instructions, but the owner was away buying fabric, and she had no way of confirming who was calling.

When Ada explained that she needed to check with the owner first, the caller became impatient and insisted that the problem required immediate attention.

She ended the call without installing anything.

Later, the owner contacted the service provider using the business's existing contact information. The provider could not confirm the supposed emergency.

They had lost time dealing with the interruption, but no remote access had been granted.

What this story teaches: A convincing caller may use a real business concern to pressure an employee into granting access. Staff should be able to delay an unfamiliar request until it has been independently verified.

Make it easier for staff to do the safe thing

A small business can reduce these risks without creating a complicated policy manual.

Identify trusted contacts. Keep a short, maintained list of support providers, bank contacts and managers who can approve sensitive requests. Protect the list against unauthorised changes.

Set clear boundaries. Tell staff which actions always require approval, especially payments, password resets, remote access, customer-data sharing and entry to restricted areas.

Make checking acceptable. Employees should be able to delay an unusual request without being blamed for taking reasonable verification steps.

Practise one conversation. During a short team briefing, read a fictional phone call aloud. Ask: What is the person claiming? What do they want? Who would we contact to check?

Keep the process current. Review contact details and access rules when providers, employees or business procedures change.

Practical takeaway: three questions before you help

Who is asking? Have I checked their identity through a contact method we already trust?

What do they want? Would the action expose money, information, an account, a device or a restricted area?

Who can authorise it? Does the request follow our business's normal approval process?

Start here

Choose one action your staff must never perform solely because someone calls or messages them. Write down who approves it and how employees should independently confirm the request. Start with remote access, account verification codes or sensitive customer records.
