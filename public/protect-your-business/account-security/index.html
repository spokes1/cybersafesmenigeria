Account Security

Know who can access your business accounts, protect the most important ones and stay in control when employees or service providers leave.

See the access map Staff departure steps

Your business has several online accounts. Who actually has the keys?

Think about the accounts your business uses every day. The bank account you receive payments into. The WhatsApp number customers contact. Your Instagram page, business email and the platform where you store invoices.

Now consider how many people can access them.

Perhaps a former employee still knows the password to your payment dashboard. A freelancer manages your business Instagram page through their personal account. Or the only person who can reset your website password is someone who helped you set it up two years ago.

These arrangements might work without problems for a long time. But when an account is compromised, a phone is lost or someone leaves the business, they can become serious obstacles.

Good account security is about more than strong passwords. It means knowing which accounts matter, who has permission to use them and how your business can regain control when something goes wrong.

Rule to remember

YOUR BUSINESS SHOULD ALWAYS KNOW WHO HAS ACCESS, WHAT THEY CAN DO AND HOW TO REMOVE THAT ACCESS.

What is account security?

Account security means protecting the online accounts your business depends on and controlling who can use them.

These might include:

  • Business email and document storage.
  • Online banking and payment platforms.
  • WhatsApp Business and other customer messaging accounts.
  • Facebook, Instagram and other business social media pages.
  • Your website, online shop and domain name.
  • Accounting, inventory, payroll or staff-management systems.

Some people need access to perform everyday tasks. Others may need additional permission to change settings, approve payments or manage other users.

A person with this higher level of permission is commonly called an administrator, or admin.

Account security involves protecting both ordinary users and administrators, while ensuring that no one has more access than their work requires.

Why should your business care?

An account can hold more than one type of valuable information

A single business account may provide access to customer conversations, payment records, stock information or confidential documents.

A former employee may still be able to sign in

If access is not removed when someone leaves, their old account or permissions may remain active.

The person who created an account may control it

If your business page or website was created through someone else's personal account, recovering control later may be difficult.

Administrative access can affect the entire business

Someone with full control of a platform may be able to change settings, remove users or alter important information.

Losing access can interrupt everyday operations

A locked social media account, payment dashboard or online store can prevent staff from serving customers and managing business activities.

Which accounts should you protect first?

Start with the accounts whose loss would cause the greatest disruption.

For many small businesses, these will be the following.

Business email

Email is often connected to password resets, business documents and other important services.

Protect it with strong sign-in methods and review account recovery settings.

Our Email Security article explains these protections in detail.

Banking and payment platforms

Protect accounts used to receive money, initiate transfers and approve transactions.

Give payment permissions only to authorised employees and enable additional authentication where supported.

If your bank offers separate user permissions or approval controls, use them rather than sharing one banking login.

WhatsApp Business

Protect the phone number and device used for the account.

Enable the available account security protections and review linked devices.

Remove linked devices that are no longer recognised or authorised.

Business social media accounts

Check who can manage your Facebook Page, Instagram account or other business profiles.

Avoid leaving full control with someone who no longer works for the business.

Where supported, use business-management tools to assign individual permissions rather than sharing passwords.

Website, domain and cloud storage

Know who controls the account used to renew your domain name, manage your website and access your business documents.

Ensure that important accounts use recovery information under the business's control.

The order may differ depending on your business. A shop selling primarily through Instagram may depend on its social media account as heavily as another business depends on its website or email.

Create a simple Account Access Map

Many businesses have never written down which accounts they own or who can access them.

You can correct this with a basic spreadsheet or securely stored document.

For each important account, record:

  • Account or service: The platform and what your business uses it for.
  • Business owner or responsible person: Who is accountable for the account.
  • People with access: Current employees or service providers who can sign in.
  • Permission level: Whether each person has ordinary or administrator access.
  • Recovery responsibility: Who can manage recovery if access is lost.
  • Last reviewed: The date access was last checked.

Do not write passwords, PINs or recovery codes in the access map.

Store those separately using an appropriate, secured password manager or another protected recovery arrangement.

The access map is meant to help you understand who controls each account, not become another place where sensitive login information is exposed.

Give each person the access they actually need

Not every employee needs full control of every business system.

For example, an employee responsible for posting products on Instagram may need permission to create content but not to remove other administrators.

A cashier may need to view payment confirmations without having the ability to initiate large transfers.

An office assistant may need access to a shared folder containing customer orders but not the business's payroll records.

This approach is called least privilege. It simply means giving people the minimum access necessary to perform their work.

Where a platform supports separate users and permissions:

  1. Create an individual account for each employee.
  2. Assign the permission level their work requires.
  3. Reserve administrator privileges for a small number of authorised people.
  4. Review permissions when responsibilities change.

Separate accounts also help you identify which user performed an action.

Avoid sharing one password with everyone

It may seem convenient to give employees the same login details for a business platform.

However, shared passwords create problems.

You may not know who changed a setting, downloaded information or approved a transaction.

When an employee leaves, everyone else may need a new password. Even then, other active sessions or connected applications might remain authorised.

Whenever possible, use individual user accounts, shared-mailbox features or delegated access.

Delegated access means allowing someone to perform approved tasks using their own account rather than giving them your password.

If a service genuinely does not support separate users, limit access to the smallest practical number of authorised people. Use a controlled password-sharing method, review active sessions and change the shared credential when access needs change.

Never send important passwords or recovery codes through an ordinary group chat.

Secure sign-ins and account recovery

Each important business account should use strong sign-in protection.

Use unique passwords or passkeys

A passkey is a sign-in method supported by some services that uses a device or credential manager to verify your identity.

Where properly supported, passkeys provide strong protection against many common phishing attacks.

For accounts that still require passwords, use strong, unique passwords and a reputable password manager.

Enable multi-factor authentication

Multi-factor authentication, or MFA, requires an additional check when signing in.

Use phishing-resistant methods, such as passkeys or security keys, where available. An authenticator app is another useful option.

SMS verification is better than relying on a password alone, but a SIM swap or phone-number compromise can create additional risks.

No authentication method removes the need to protect devices and recovery settings.

Protect your recovery options

Check that recovery email addresses, phone numbers and backup methods are current and controlled by authorised people.

Where a service provides recovery codes, store them securely somewhere you can access even if your main account or phone is unavailable.

A protected password manager or a physical copy in a locked location may be appropriate, depending on the business.

Do not leave recovery codes in an ordinary shared folder or with general business documents.

Plan for the loss of an administrator

For important systems that support multiple administrators, consider having another trusted and separately secured administrator who can help recover access.

This should be someone with a legitimate business responsibility, not simply anyone available.

Keep the number of full administrators limited, and protect their accounts carefully.

The goal is to avoid a situation where losing one person's phone or account leaves the business unable to manage its own systems.

What should happen when an employee leaves?

Removing access should be a normal part of employee departures and changes in responsibility.

It should not depend on whether the person left on good terms.

Step 1: Identify every account they used

Check the Account Access Map, business email, cloud storage, payment platforms, social media accounts and any service-provider accounts.

Include contractors and freelancers who previously worked with the business.

Step 2: Arrange the handover of business information

Before permanently deleting an account, determine whether important documents, customer conversations or work records need to be transferred or preserved.

Follow your provider's account-management process and any applicable record-retention requirements.

Step 3: Revoke access promptly

Disable the person's individual account or remove their permissions at the appropriate time.

Where supported, sign out existing sessions and revoke relevant application access.

Removing access from one platform does not necessarily remove it from other connected services.

Step 4: Secure any shared credentials

If the departing employee knew a shared password, change it and review active sessions.

Check administrator accounts and recovery methods to ensure the departing person no longer controls them.

Step 5: Review business devices and connected applications

Recover business-owned devices where appropriate, remove authorised device access and check whether any third-party integrations still have permission to use the business account.

Step 6: Update your access records

Record who now manages the account and when permissions were changed.

A simple documented process can prevent old access arrangements from being forgotten.

Warning signs that account access needs attention

Review an account when you notice:

  • A former employee or contractor still appearing in the user or administrator list.
  • A business account connected to an unknown or outdated recovery phone number.
  • Login alerts or active sessions you cannot explain.
  • New administrator permissions that nobody authorised.
  • Unexpected changes to business profile information or payment settings.
  • A service provider who is the only person able to manage or recover an account.

An unfamiliar sign-in location alone does not prove compromise. Some legitimate services and connections can produce unexpected location information.

Look at the activity and circumstances before drawing conclusions.

What should you do if someone gains access without permission?

If an account appears to be compromised, focus on containing further access and protecting the business.

1. Use the service's official recovery or security process

Open the genuine application or website directly.

Change the password or follow the provider's account-recovery process if you can no longer sign in.

2. Remove unauthorised access

Review active sessions, connected devices, administrator permissions and linked applications.

Revoke suspicious access using the provider's available controls.

If the account is managed by an administrator, ask them to investigate and apply the appropriate restrictions.

3. Secure recovery methods

Check whether anyone changed the account's recovery phone number, email address or authentication settings.

Restore authorised settings and save new recovery methods securely.

4. Check what happened

Review available activity records, messages, account changes and transactions.

Preserve relevant evidence, including notifications, screenshots and transaction references.

Avoid deleting records that may be needed to investigate the incident.

5. Protect affected customers and business partners

If the account was used to send fraudulent messages or alter payment instructions, inform affected people through a separate, trusted communication channel.

Ask them to verify unusual requests before acting.

6. Report suspected financial fraud or cybercrime

If unauthorised payments have occurred, contact your bank or payment provider immediately.

Where appropriate, report suspected cybercrime through the Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre:

https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/

For incidents involving business systems or information, seek assistance from your service provider or a qualified cybersecurity professional.

Regaining control or recovering lost funds cannot be guaranteed.

Make access reviews part of normal business operations

You don't need a large IT department to manage account access responsibly.

For a small team, a monthly review of important accounts is a practical starting point.

Check whether:

  • The account owner and recovery contact are still correct.
  • Every person with access still needs it.
  • Administrator permissions are limited to appropriate people.
  • Former staff or contractors have been removed.
  • Unfamiliar devices, sessions or integrations need investigation.

For businesses with staff changes or many external service providers, review access whenever those changes happen rather than waiting for the next monthly check.

The Instagram account nobody had properly handed over

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

For several months, a small clothing business in Lagos had relied on a freelance assistant, Mariam, to manage its Instagram promotions and respond to customer enquiries.

When their working arrangement ended, the owner, Dara, thanked her for her work and assumed that was the end of it.

Two weeks later, Dara wanted to launch a new promotion. While checking the account settings, she noticed that Mariam still had access to some of the business's social media tools.

Dara considered removing her immediately, but then realised something else. Mariam had helped set up several of the promotional tools, and Dara wasn't entirely sure which accounts and permissions belonged to the business.

She was worried that changing the wrong setting might interrupt the campaign.

Dara contacted Mariam to arrange a proper handover. They reviewed the account permissions, identified the business's authorised administrators and checked the available recovery options before removing access that was no longer needed.

The new promotion was delayed while the details were sorted out.

There was no evidence that Mariam had misused the account. The problem was simply that the business had never treated digital access as part of the working arrangement.

What this story teaches: When employees or freelancers leave, businesses need to review account ownership, permissions and recovery access. A proper handover matters just as much as returning physical equipment.

  2. Dara finds old permissions
  3. Owner and Mariam arrange a handover
  4. Authorised access and recovery checked before removal

Your Account Access Map: Know who holds the keys

For your three most important business accounts, answer the following:

Who owns it?
Name the person accountable for the account.

Who can use it?
Identify current employees, contractors and service providers with access.

Who can change its settings?
Identify users with administrator or full-control permissions.

Who can recover it?
Confirm that recovery information and any backup administrator access remain under appropriate business control.

When was access last reviewed?
Record the review date and any changes required.

You can extend this simple record to the rest of your business accounts over time.
