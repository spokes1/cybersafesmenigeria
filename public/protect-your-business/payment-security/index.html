Payment Security

Simple ways to receive payments safely, verify supplier account details and prevent costly payment mistakes in your business.

See the payment verification guide Staff checklist

You know who you're paying. But are you sure the account is theirs?

You have been buying goods from the same supplier for months. Today, an invoice arrives with a different bank account number. The message explains that the supplier has changed banks and wants you to use the new details.

Everything else looks familiar. The business name is correct, the amount matches your order and the message appears to come from someone you regularly deal with.

Would you make the transfer?

This is one of the everyday payment decisions that can expose a business to fraud. The same risk exists when customers present transfer receipts, staff handle payments in a rush or a business fails to notice an incorrect transaction.

You don't need a complicated financial system to improve payment security. You need clear checks that everyone follows before accepting or sending money.

Rule to remember

MONEY COMING IN? CONFIRM THE CREDIT. MONEY GOING OUT? VERIFY THE RECIPIENT.

What is payment security?

Payment security means putting simple controls in place to help your business receive money, make payments and handle financial transactions safely.

For a small business, this includes checking whether customer payments have arrived, confirming the correct recipient before transferring money, protecting banking accounts and keeping reliable transaction records.

It also means ensuring that employees understand when a payment can proceed and when they should stop and ask for confirmation.

These practices matter whether you operate a retail shop, restaurant, small office, online store or service business.

Why should your business care?

  • One incorrect transfer can affect your cash flow. Money sent to the wrong account may be difficult to recover, especially if the payment was fraudulent.
  • Payment requests can come from familiar contacts. A message appearing to come from a supplier, employee or business partner may have been sent by an impersonator or through a compromised account.
  • Staff decisions affect payment safety. A worker who accepts an unverified receipt or follows an unexpected payment instruction can unintentionally expose the business to loss.
  • Poor records make problems harder to resolve. Without transaction references, invoices and approval records, it can be difficult to establish what happened.
  • Payment mistakes can damage business relationships. An unpaid supplier, delayed customer order or disputed transaction can consume time and disrupt normal operations.

Where do payment problems usually happen?

Customers presenting unconfirmed transfers

A customer may show a successful transfer receipt even though your business has not received the money.

Sometimes, a legitimate payment is delayed. In other cases, the receipt may be misleading or deliberately falsified.

The safe approach is the same: check your business's official banking records before releasing goods or completing the transaction.

For a detailed explanation, see our Fake Bank Alerts guide.

Suppliers changing their bank account details

A business receives an invoice or WhatsApp message stating that future payments should go to a different account.

The request may be legitimate. However, fraudsters can impersonate suppliers or interfere with email conversations to redirect payments.

A familiar sender name, existing message thread or correct invoice number does not establish that the new banking details are genuine.

Employees following urgent payment instructions

Someone claiming to be the business owner or a senior colleague asks an employee to make an immediate transfer.

The message might say the payment is confidential or that there is no time to follow the normal approval process.

Urgency is not a substitute for authorisation.

Transactions that are not properly recorded

A business makes several transfers during the day but fails to match them with invoices or sales records.

This can make it easier to overlook duplicate payments, incorrect amounts, unauthorised transfers and unsettled customer transactions.

Warning signs that a payment needs extra verification

Pay closer attention when:

  • A regular supplier suddenly provides different bank account details.
  • The payment request comes from a new phone number or unfamiliar email address.
  • Someone insists that a transfer must happen immediately and discourages further checks.
  • The recipient account name does not match the business or person you expected to pay.
  • A customer presents a payment screenshot, but you cannot find the corresponding credit in your own records.
  • A payment request contains an unexpected amount, invoice number or instruction to send money to another person.

These signs are reasons to investigate. They do not automatically establish that fraud has occurred.

How to receive customer payments safely

When customers pay by bank transfer, use a simple verification routine.

Step 1: Check your official banking channel

Open your bank's official app, online banking platform or merchant dashboard yourself.

Do not rely on an SMS notification, a forwarded screenshot or a link sent by the customer.

Step 2: Match the payment to the sale

Check the amount, transaction details and whether the expected incoming credit is recorded as received.

If several customers have paid similar amounts, make sure you are identifying the correct transaction rather than an earlier credit.

Step 3: Confirm before completing the sale

Release goods or provide the service according to your business's payment policy once the payment has been independently confirmed.

If you cannot verify the credit, explain politely that the transaction must remain pending.

A customer whose account was debited may need to contact their bank to trace a delayed transfer. Avoid accusing them of fraud simply because the money has not yet arrived.

What about POS and card payments?

Check the actual transaction status through your payment terminal or authorised merchant system. Do not rely solely on a photograph of a receipt.

If a payment is pending or the terminal reports an error, use your provider's official transaction history or support process to determine the next step.

Also review settlement records so you can identify payments that have not been properly accounted for.

How to verify supplier payments before sending money

This is particularly important when paying for stock, equipment, rent or other business expenses.

Step 1: Check the invoice

Confirm that the goods or services, amount and payment terms match what your business agreed to.

If something has changed unexpectedly, ask for clarification before proceeding.

Step 2: Check the recipient

Compare the bank name, account number and displayed account name with the supplier details your business already has on record.

A matching account name can be a useful check, but it is not sufficient proof that a request is genuine.

Step 3: Independently verify account changes

If a supplier provides new bank details, contact them through a phone number you already know or have independently established as genuine.

Do not rely on a phone number contained in the same email or WhatsApp message requesting the change.

Confirm the new account information with an authorised contact and record the verification.

Step 4: Obtain the appropriate approval

For larger or unusual payments, have another authorised person check the details before the transfer is made.

If you operate the business alone, use a deliberate second review of the recipient and amount before authorising the transaction.

Step 5: Record the payment

Keep the invoice, approval, payment reference and confirmation. This will help you resolve disputes and reconcile your records.

If the recipient details cannot be verified, put the payment on hold until the uncertainty is resolved.

Set up a payment routine your staff can follow

Payment security works best when it becomes part of normal business operations.

Decide who can confirm payments

Identify which employees are allowed to check incoming transfers and approve the release of goods.

If staff cannot access the appropriate verification system, make sure they know whom to contact.

Avoid giving every employee unrestricted access to the business's main bank account.

Set an approval limit

Choose a payment amount above which a second authorised person must approve the transfer.

The limit should reflect your business's normal expenses and financial capacity.

Where your banking platform supports separate users and approvals, consider using those features rather than sharing one login.

Protect access to business banking

Use a strong, unique password and enable multi-factor authentication where available.

Never share banking passwords, PINs, one-time passwords or authorisation codes through WhatsApp, email or ordinary phone calls.

Remove access promptly when an employee leaves or no longer needs it.

Keep a verified supplier list

Maintain a secure record of your regular suppliers, their approved payment details and trusted contact numbers.

Any change to this record should be independently verified and approved.

Review your transactions regularly

For a busy retail business, checking transactions at the end of each day is a useful habit.

Match incoming payments against sales and outgoing transfers against invoices. Investigate unexplained amounts, missing credits and unexpected debits promptly.

Even a small business using a basic spreadsheet or transaction book can benefit from this routine.

What should you do if something goes wrong?

The first actions depend on whether money has actually left your account.

If you sent money to a suspected fraudulent account

  1. Contact your bank immediately. Use an official support or fraud-reporting channel. Explain what happened and ask what tracing, recall or other recovery measures may be available.
  2. Preserve your records. Keep the transaction reference, amount, date, beneficiary details, invoice, messages and any evidence showing how the payment was authorised.
  3. Notify the business owner or responsible manager. Stop further payments related to the suspicious instruction until the details are verified.
  4. Secure any compromised accounts. If the request may have come through a hacked email or WhatsApp account, change affected passwords from a trusted device, review active sessions and enable additional account protection.
  5. Report suspected fraud to the authorities. The Nigeria Police Force National Cybercrime Centre provides a reporting portal at https://nccc.npf.gov.ng/ereport/.

Recovery depends on the circumstances. Reporting quickly may help an investigation, but it does not guarantee that the money will be returned.

If a customer's payment is missing

Check your official account records and payment provider's transaction history.

If you cannot identify a matching credit, keep the sale pending and ask the customer to contact their sending bank or payment provider.

You can also raise a payment enquiry through your receiving bank's official support channel.

If your bank does not resolve a complaint

Keep the bank's complaint or tracking reference and your supporting documents.

The Central Bank of Nigeria provides a process for escalating eligible unresolved complaints against regulated financial institutions through its Consumer Protection Department.

A banking complaint is different from reporting suspected criminal fraud. Depending on the circumstances, both may be appropriate.

Keep payment records without exposing sensitive information

A useful payment record should include the date, amount, invoice or order reference, payment status and the person who verified or authorised it.

Keep records in a location accessible only to staff who need them.

Do not store customers' banking passwords, PINs, one-time passwords or unnecessary financial information.

If you maintain a supplier payment register or spreadsheet, protect it against unauthorised changes and keep a backup.

The supplier who received two payment instructions

Illustrative scenario. This is not an account of a real incident.

It was Saturday morning, and a small catering business in Ibadan was preparing food for a wedding reception.

The owner, Bisi, needed extra cooking supplies from a regular distributor. She attempted a bank transfer, but the banking application showed that the transaction was still processing.

The distributor called to say that no payment confirmation had arrived and that the supplies would not be released until payment was received.

Read the full storyShow less1-minute read

Bisi was already dealing with the kitchen team and an impatient event organiser. She asked her assistant, Femi, to follow up.

Femi checked the supplier's details and made another transfer, believing the first one had failed.

Neither of them recorded that a second payment had been attempted.

Later that afternoon, Bisi reviewed the business account and noticed two debits for the same amount.

She called Femi, who explained what he had done. They contacted the bank and supplier to establish the status of both transactions.

The money had left the business account, but they could not immediately confirm how the payments would be reconciled. That left less money available for other wedding expenses.

There had been no suspicious email, impersonated supplier or fraudulent request. The problem came from uncertainty about a pending transaction and unclear responsibility for authorising another payment.

Afterwards, Bisi agreed that staff would check unresolved transactions through the bank's official records and obtain approval before repeating a transfer.

What this story teaches: Not every payment loss or disruption begins with a scam. Unclear transaction status, duplicate payments and poor approval procedures can also put a small business under financial pressure.
