Payment Security

Almost all payment fraud against small businesses here comes down to unverified confirmations and changed account details.

Rule one: confirm the credit, not the notification

Goods do not leave until the money is visible in your own account, checked by you in your own banking app. A screenshot is an image. An SMS sender name is text. Neither is proof.

  • Match the exact amount and the exact time, not just "a credit today".
  • Watch for reused screenshots from earlier legitimate transactions.
  • Expect pressure at closing time and during rushes. That is when this is attempted.

Rule two: verify every change of account details

A request to pay into a different account is confirmed by phone, on a number you already had, with a person you know. Not the number in the email. Not a WhatsApp reply. A call you place.

This single rule defeats business email compromise, supplier impersonation and most invoice fraud. It costs two minutes.

Set up the business so the rules hold

  1. Name one person who confirms incoming payments, and make it clear no staff member is blamed for making a customer wait.
  2. Require a second approver for transfers above a threshold you set.
  3. Keep a written list of supplier account details, and treat any change as an exception requiring a call.
  4. Turn on transaction alerts for every business account so unexpected activity is visible immediately.
  5. Reconcile weekly. Fraud found in seven days is sometimes recoverable; fraud found in a month usually is not.

If a fraudulent payment has gone out

  1. Call your bank immediately and ask them to flag and attempt a recall. Hours matter.
  2. Preserve the invoice, the email thread, the account details and the transaction reference.
  3. Report to the EFCC (opens in a new tab) with your evidence, and to the police.
  4. Then look at how the request arrived. If it came from inside an email thread, assume the mailbox is compromised and secure it.
