Device Security.

Keep the laptops, shared computers, phones and payment devices your business depends on protected through practical everyday habits.

The laptop serves customers. Who keeps it safe?

The small laptop beside your counter does more than open spreadsheets. Staff use it to check orders, download customer files, sign in to business email and sometimes confirm payments. A phone at the same counter may carry WhatsApp Business chats, customer addresses and access to important accounts.

Now imagine the next shift starts and nobody can remember who last installed an application, which employee is still signed in or whether the computer has received security updates. The machine may work perfectly well, but the business doesn't have a clear answer to some important security questions.

Device security doesn't begin with buying expensive software. It begins with knowing which devices matter, who may use them and how they are kept protected throughout an ordinary working day.

RULE TO REMEMBER:

KEEP WORK DEVICES UPDATED, LOCKED, CONTROLLED AND RECOVERABLE.

What is device security?

Device security means protecting the physical devices used for business and the information and accounts they can reach.

It includes computers in a shop or office, personal phones authorised for business work, tablets used for bookings, and devices connected to business systems. Some payment terminals and specialist equipment have security settings managed by their providers. Ask the provider about approved updates and access rather than attempting unsupported changes yourself.

The aim is not to make every employee a computer technician. It is to agree on sensible settings and responsibilities so an unfamiliar download, missing phone or former employee's login doesn't quietly become a bigger problem.

A secure device is not necessarily an uncompromised device. Good settings reduce risk, but no single application or feature guarantees protection.

Why should a small business care?

One device may hold several business functions. The same laptop might contain customer measurements, supplier invoices, saved browser sessions and access to an online store. Losing control of it can affect multiple parts of the business.

Shared devices make responsibility unclear. If five staff members use one desktop account, it becomes harder to know who changed settings, installed software or left a service signed in.

Old equipment may still work but lack protection. A computer can continue switching on after its operating system stops receiving ordinary security updates. Functioning is not the same as being supported.

Lost devices can expose information. Someone who finds an unlocked phone may be able to read business messages or attempt account access. A screen lock and appropriate encryption can reduce the risk, although neither replaces prompt account protection.

An interruption has a business cost. A device taken out of use for assessment can delay orders, records and communication, even before anyone knows whether there has been a cyberattack.

Start with the devices that matter most

Write down the devices used to run your business. For each one, identify its purpose, who is allowed to use it and who is responsible for arranging updates or repairs.

You could begin with:

  • The shop's shared computer or office laptop.
  • The phone receiving WhatsApp Business orders and delivery calls.
  • The owner's or accountant's computer used for finance and records.
  • Tablets for bookings, stock checks or customer service.
  • Provider-managed point-of-sale terminals or other specialist equipment.

Do not publish this information or expose device login details. A simple private notebook or controlled spreadsheet is enough to begin.

If an employee uses a personal phone for authorised work, agree what business information may be stored on it, how access will be removed when their role changes and whom they should notify if it is lost. Don't assume that the business can inspect or erase an employee's personal information without proper authorisation.

Six security checks

Pick a check to focus on. All six are explained below.

  5. 05

  6. 06

    What to check: Could key records be restored without this device?

    What to change: Keep protected backups and periodically test recovery.

This walkthrough helps you recognise useful controls. It cannot confirm whether your own device is secure.

The six everyday checks

1. Check that operating systems and applications are supported and updated

Turn on automatic updates where available for Windows, macOS, Android, iPhone and the important applications used for business. Restart devices when updates require it, and set aside a suitable time to check that updates actually completed.

Browsers, office tools, PDF readers and business software also need attention. A fully updated computer with an unsupported application may still be exposed.

Important for older Windows computers: Windows 10 support ended on 14 October 2025. If your business still uses Windows 10, start planning to move to Windows 11. Check whether your existing computers meet the Windows 11 requirements and, where they do not, plan for replacement with supported devices. Back up important business files before making the change.

If internet data is expensive, schedule downloads when a trusted, affordable connection is available. Delaying essential updates indefinitely is not a good long-term plan.

2. Lock devices and protect stored information

Set a strong sign-in password or an appropriate PIN and enable automatic screen locking after a short idle period. Biometrics can be convenient, but maintain a secure recovery method.

Keep devices physically secure. Don't leave a business laptop unattended on a counter where customers can use it. Protect phones against casual access, especially when they contain customer messages and banking-related applications.

Use device or disk encryption where supported. This helps protect data on a lost or stolen device that is properly locked and powered off. It doesn't stop someone from reading information on an already unlocked computer.

On supported Windows PCs, check whether Device encryption is available. BitLocker Drive Encryption has additional edition requirements on Windows Pro, Enterprise and Education. Macs support FileVault. Save recovery keys in an appropriate, secure place controlled by the business, separate from the device they unlock. Don't enable encryption and then lose the only recovery key.

3. Give each worker appropriate access

For shared desktop computers, separate standard user accounts are preferable to everybody using the same administrator login. Staff should normally work with accounts that cannot change high-risk settings or install arbitrary software without approval.

Keep administrator privileges for someone responsible for maintenance and recovery. Record who controls them and how the business can recover access if that person leaves.

When staff change jobs or leave, remove permissions they no longer need and review browser sessions, connected applications and business accounts. For detailed permission and staff-handover guidance, see Account Security.

If the device or application cannot support individual logins, agree on who can use it, restrict what it can access and provide an explicit sign-out and handover procedure. A shared login should not become a reason to share all business passwords.

4. Control new software and file transfers

Install applications from their genuine publisher, the operating system's official store or an approved supplier. Be cautious with adverts posing as download buttons, cracked software, installers shared in chat groups and unexpected update prompts.

If staff need a new application to finish a customer's job, they should know who can approve it and where to obtain it. The rule should help them get work done safely, not leave them stranded when something urgent happens.

Treat unknown USB drives as untrusted. Avoid connecting a device of uncertain origin to an important business computer. If you must receive files through removable media, have them checked using trusted security controls, without executing unfamiliar programmes. A scan reduces some risks but cannot guarantee safety.

Do not upload customer invoices, identification documents, private records or confidential files to a public malware-analysis website as a routine safety check. Standard VirusTotal uploads are not the same as its separate private-scanning service and may be shared within a wider security-analysis ecosystem. Use approved local or managed security tools and obtain appropriate advice when a file is suspicious.

Cloud sharing is also not automatically safe. Use trusted services, appropriate access permissions and sensible expiry or revocation of shared links.

5. Keep existing security protection enabled

Use the device's supported built-in security features or reputable security software appropriate to the business. On a supported Windows PC, Windows Security / Microsoft Defender Antivirus and the firewall provide important built-in protections when configured and updated. A larger or more complex business may need additional managed controls.

Don't disable a security alert to make an unfamiliar installer run. Treat unexpected prompts as a reason to stop and check.

Review unwanted browser extensions, unfamiliar applications and permissions. Remove software that is no longer required through the normal, supported process.

Phones also contain platform-specific protection features. Follow the official guidance for Android or iPhone, rather than installing a random app advertised as an instant virus cleaner.

6. Prepare for loss, repair and recovery

Keep important business information backed up in an approved, protected location that can be accessed without the original device. Do not leave the only backup drive permanently attached to the computer it protects. Test restoration when practical.

Know who can recover the device account, where authorised encryption recovery keys are held, and which business systems must be secured if the device disappears.

Before taking a computer or phone for repair, back up what you can safely, sign out of sensitive sessions where appropriate, and choose a trusted service provider. Discuss how the repairer will handle personal or customer information. Never provide more credentials than necessary.

Before selling or disposing of a device, preserve required business records, remove business accounts through supported procedures and securely erase or reset it using the manufacturer's guidance. Simply deleting files may not remove all their contents.

WORKPLACE EXAMPLE · ILLUSTRATIVE

A special routine for the shared counter computer

Consider a small shop where three employees use one computer for stock checks and customer correspondence. The owner has no dedicated IT employee. One person starts the morning shift, another takes over after lunch and the third closes the shop.

What happens if the morning worker leaves WhatsApp Web or email open? The next worker may see messages they aren't meant to handle, and customers could approach a computer that's still unlocked.

They don't need an elaborate monitoring system to improve this. The owner can assign standard accounts where possible, explain who may install software and agree on a simple end-of-shift routine: save work to the right place, sign out of sensitive sessions, remove unnecessary files from the desktop and lock the screen.

The employees still need clear rules about which records they may access. A screen lock alone won't solve excessive permissions or shared-password problems.

The lesson: A shared device can be practical for a small business, but shared responsibility needs explicit rules. This is a workplace example, not a report of an actual incident.

What to check on the device you already use

Windows 11 computer

  • Open Start → Settings → Windows Update and check the update status. Let pending updates finish and restart when prompted.
  • Open Start → Windows Security and check that virus and threat protection and the firewall are active. If a business administrator manages the device, follow their instructions.
  • Check Settings → Accounts to understand which users have access. Create separate standard accounts where practical, with the owner retaining suitable recovery access.
  • Search Windows settings for Device encryption. Availability depends on the hardware, edition and configuration. Keep any recovery key secure.

Menus vary between versions and managed-device policies. Don't bypass organisation-managed restrictions.

Mac computer

  • Open System Settings → General → Software Update to check operating-system updates.
  • Use System Settings → Lock Screen to review automatic locking and password requirements.
  • Check Users & Groups and keep administrator access limited.
  • Where appropriate, review Privacy & Security → FileVault and the recovery arrangements before enabling it.

Android and iPhone

Verify the operating-system update status, screen lock, application permissions and supported device-finding or remote-lock features. Exact settings vary across Android brands and operating-system versions. If personal and business accounts coexist on one device, plan how to protect both.

For full platform-specific instructions and a lost-phone response, use our Mobile Security article rather than trying to reproduce every manufacturer's menu here.

What if a work device is missing or acting suspiciously?

If the device is missing: Inform the responsible person immediately. Use the official device-finding or remote-lock service if it was enabled and the business is authorised to use it. Review sessions and protect affected accounts. Contact the mobile network provider promptly if the missing phone holds the business number. If personal data may be exposed, assess the appropriate privacy response. Remote erase may be appropriate in some circumstances, but can destroy information needed for recovery or investigation, so consider the consequences.

If you suspect malicious software or unauthorised access: Stop sensitive activity, avoid installing random cleaners, disconnect the affected device from the network where safe, preserve useful information and seek trusted assistance. Secure potentially exposed accounts from another known-safe device. Use our Malware and Incident Response guides for detailed steps.

If the device has simply stopped working: Don't assume that every fault is an attack. Preserve the device and available records, identify an appropriate support route and restore business work through verified backups and unaffected equipment if safe.

A routine that takes less than a meeting

01 · OPENUse the assigned account

Check that the right worker signs in and that the computer is ready for work.

02 · DURINGKeep work in approved places

Use approved applications and handle customer files carefully.

03 · CLOSESign out and lock

Save records, close sensitive sessions and leave the screen locked.

Use a short opening or closing check at the shop or office:

  1. Is the computer locked when nobody is using it?
  2. Are there pending security updates or genuine warnings someone needs to review?
  3. Is the right employee using the right account, with unnecessary administrator access avoided?
  4. Has anybody installed unfamiliar software or received a suspicious USB drive?
  5. Have customer documents been stored in the approved place rather than copied to random personal folders?
  6. Are sensitive email, messaging and banking sessions signed out when the shift ends?
  7. Do the owner and staff know whom to notify if the device goes missing or appears compromised?

You do not need to inspect all seven items every hour. Use them to establish a practical routine and periodically review the devices the business relies on.

START HERE

Choose the single device your business would struggle to operate without. Today, check its update status, screen lock, user access and backup arrangements. Then assign one person to make sure these checks are repeated.
